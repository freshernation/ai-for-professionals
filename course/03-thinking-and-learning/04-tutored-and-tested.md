# Being tutored and tested instead of lectured

## Outcome

After this lesson you can turn your assistant into a tutor that questions you, finds where your understanding breaks down, and tests you, instead of one that explains at you.

## Why it matters

Ask an assistant to explain something and you get a clear, well-organised explanation. You read it and nod, and you feel that you understand. A week later you cannot reproduce it, and when you try to apply it you find a gap you did not know was there. Reading an explanation feels like learning and often is not. Learning sticks when you have to produce the answer yourself and someone notices where you go wrong. A good tutor does that. An assistant can do it too, at any hour and with endless patience, but only if you ask, because its default is to lecture.

## The idea

**Teacher and tutor**

A teacher explains the material to a group. A tutor works with one person and finds that person's particular confusion. One-to-one tutoring has long been regarded as the most effective form of teaching. In a well-known 1984 paper, the educational researcher Benjamin Bloom reported that tutored students did dramatically better than those taught in an ordinary class. Later reviews found a smaller effect than his, and still a large one. The trouble was always cost, and that constraint has now largely gone.

**Why being tested works**

Pulling something out of your memory strengthens it more than putting it in again. In a much-cited 2006 experiment by the psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke, students who tested themselves on a passage remembered more of it a week later than students who had spent the same time re-reading it. The re-readers, though, felt more confident. That is the trap. Re-reading and listening to explanations feel productive. Being questioned feels harder and works better.

**The tutor prompt**

You have to switch the assistant out of explaining mode. Four instructions do it:

```
Be my tutor on [topic]. I'm learning it so that I can [purpose].

- Ask me one question at a time and wait for my answer.
- Don't lecture. Don't explain until I've had a go.
- Your aim is to find the gaps in my understanding.
- Be precise about what I got right and what I got wrong. Don't
  praise me unless I've earned it.
```

Each line blocks a habit:

| Instruction | What it prevents |
|---|---|
| One question at a time | A list of questions you skim and half answer |
| Don't lecture | The default: a tidy explanation you read passively |
| Find the gaps | Questions about what you already know, which feel good and teach nothing |
| Be precise | "Great answer!" followed by a correction you barely notice |

**Two short commands**

For everyday use, two phrases cover most of what you need:

- **"Teach me [X]"** asks for an explanation. Use it when something is new to you.
- **"Test me on [X]"** makes you produce the answers. Use it once you think you understand.

Move between the two. Learn a little, then get tested on it, and go back to the explanation only where the testing shows a gap.

**Other ways to make yourself do the work**

- **Explain it back.** "I'm going to explain [concept] in my own words. Tell me what I've got wrong or left out." Teaching something is the hardest test of understanding it.
- **Apply it.** "Give me a realistic situation from [my field] where I'd need to use this, and let me work through it."
- **Find the edge.** "Keep making the questions harder until I get one wrong."
- **Come back later.** "Test me on what we covered last week." Returning after a gap is when the testing does most good.

Tutoring also works well aloud, in your assistant's voice mode.

**When you get a question wrong**

A wrong answer has found something you can now fix. Ask the assistant to explain that point only, then to test you on it again in a different form.

**One caution**

The assistant can be wrong too. For specialised, recent or contested material, give it your sources and tell it to tutor you from those.

## Worked example

Sofia is a finance analyst at a retail chain. She has been asked to evaluate a programme in which the twenty worst-performing stores received extra management coaching. Sales at those stores rose 8% the following quarter, and the operations team wants to roll the programme out to every store. A colleague mentioned "regression to the mean" in passing. Sofia read an explanation of it and thinks she understands. She decides to find out.

```
Be my tutor on regression to the mean. I'm a finance analyst and need
to judge whether a coaching programme for underperforming stores
really worked.

- Ask me one question at a time and wait for my answer.
- Don't lecture. Don't explain until I've had a go.
- Your aim is to find the gaps in my understanding.
- Be precise about what I got right and wrong.
```

> **Assistant:** In your own words, what is regression to the mean?
>
> **Sofia:** Extreme results tend to be followed by more average ones.
>
> **Assistant:** Correct as far as it goes. Why does that happen?
>
> **Sofia:** Because things even out over time?
>
> **Assistant:** That is the gap. "Evening out" suggests a force pulling results back, and there is no such force. Try this: a store's quarterly sales depend partly on lasting factors, such as location and management, and partly on luck that quarter, such as weather or a local event. If you pick the twenty stores with the worst quarter, what can you say about their luck that quarter?
>
> **Sofia:** It was probably bad. Worse than usual.
>
> **Assistant:** Yes. And next quarter, what would you expect their luck to be?
>
> **Sofia:** Average. So they would improve anyway, even if we did nothing.
>
> **Assistant:** Exactly. So what does the 8% rise tell you about the coaching?
>
> **Sofia:** Not much on its own. I need to know how much they would have improved without it.
>
> **Assistant:** How could you find that out from data you already hold?

Sofia works out that she can look at the twenty worst stores in earlier years, when there was no programme, and see how much they improved the following quarter.

She had read an explanation and believed she understood. The second question showed she held the wrong picture, the kind that would have let the 8% stand as evidence. When she runs the comparison, the worst twenty stores in the two previous years improved by 5% and 6% the next quarter with no coaching at all. Her report says the programme's real effect is perhaps two or three points, and recommends a proper trial before a full rollout.

## Try it on your own work

1. Pick a concept you have recently read about or "sort of" understand, and that matters for your work.
2. Use the tutor prompt. Answer every question before reading on.
3. Keep going until you get one wrong. Note what the gap was.
4. Ask for an explanation of that point only, then ask to be tested on it in a different form.
5. Tomorrow, open a chat and say "Test me on [the concept]" without re-reading anything.
6. If your assistant has a voice mode, try step 2 aloud.

## Ignore for now

- Flashcard apps and formal study systems. "Test me" covers most needs.
- Worrying about looking foolish. Nobody is watching, and wrong answers are what you are after.
