# Learning a new subject: a plan, a sequence, and milestones

## Outcome

After this lesson you can have an assistant build you a personal learning plan for any subject: one that starts from what you already know, runs in a sensible order, tells you what to skip, and lets you prove you are making progress.

## Why it matters

Professionals constantly need to learn things outside their training: enough finance to challenge a budget, enough data analysis to question a dashboard, enough about a new market to hold a conversation. The usual approach is to buy a course or a book, start at chapter one, and stop by chapter three. The material was written for everyone, so half of it is too basic or beside the point for you, and nothing tells you whether you are getting anywhere. A personal plan fixes that. Building one used to need a mentor with time to spare. It now takes a twenty-minute conversation.

## The idea

A good learning plan makes five decisions. A good teacher or mentor makes them for you. You can have an assistant make them with you.

**1. Destination: what will you be able to do?**

State an ability, with its purpose attached. "Learn about pricing" has no end. "Be able to propose and defend a price for our new product tier to the commercial director" tells you what to study and when you are finished. Once the end point is an ability, each week of the plan can be justified by asking whether it gets you closer to it.

**2. Baseline: where are you starting?**

A plan that does not know your starting point has to guess, and it usually guesses too low. You probably know more than nothing. You may also have gaps in things you believe you know. Have the assistant find out, and don't try to describe your level yourself.

**3. Sequence: in what order?**

The same topics in a different order can be far harder to learn. Some ideas only make sense after others. A good sequence puts the foundations first and gets you doing something real early, so that the learning has something to attach to.

**4. Ignore list: what are you leaving out for now?**

Every subject has depths that do not matter for your purpose. A plan that tries to cover everything will not be finished. An explicit list of what you are *not* learning keeps the plan small enough to complete, and stops you feeling guilty about the chapters you skipped.

**5. Milestones: how will you know?**

Each stage should end with something you produce or do: explain a concept without notes, analyse a real example, make a decision and justify it. Finishing the reading only shows that you turned the pages. A milestone you can pass or fail turns progress into a fact.

**The prompt**

Have the assistant question you before it writes anything:

```
Build me a [number]-week plan for learning [subject]. I can give it
about [hours] a week. The point is to be able to [what you want to
be able to do, and why].

The plan should have:
- the topics in order, week by week, with a reason for the order;
- a list of what to leave out at this stage;
- one milestone per week: something I produce or do that shows I've
  got it;
- two or three recommended sources per week.

Don't write it yet. First question me, one question at a time,
waiting for each answer. Find out:
- exactly what I need to be able to do at the end;
- what I already know (test me with a few questions; don't just ask
  me to rate myself);
- how much time I really have.
```

Note the line "test me with a few questions". People are poor judges of their own level. A few real questions find the baseline much more accurately than "I'd say intermediate".

**Then get it out of the chat**

A plan you never see again will not be followed. Put the study sessions in your calendar as appointments, and save the plan in a document you will open again. It takes five minutes.

**Keep the plan alive**

At the end of each week, return to the same chat, report what you did and how the milestone went, and ask the assistant to adjust the plan.

## Worked example

Lucas is a product marketing manager at a software company. The company is introducing a new premium tier, and he has been asked to recommend its price. He has never done pricing work. He has six weeks before the recommendation is due, and perhaps three hours a week.

He uses the prompt, giving the purpose as "to recommend and defend a price for a new premium software tier to our commercial director."

The assistant interviews him. Shortened:

> **Assistant:** When you present the recommendation, what will the commercial director most want to see?
>
> **Lucas:** A number, the reasoning, and what happens to existing customers.
>
> **Assistant:** Let me check where you are starting. If a product costs you 40 to deliver and you sell it at 100, and you are considering a 10% price cut, roughly how many more units must you sell to make the same profit?
>
> **Lucas:** About 10% more?
>
> **Assistant:** It is 20% more. That tells me where to begin. Next: how do you think customers currently decide whether your product is worth its price?
>
> **Lucas:** They compare us to two competitors, mostly on features.

After six questions the assistant produces a plan. In summary:

- **Week 1:** How price, volume and margin interact. *Milestone: work out, without help, the break-even volume for three price changes on your own product.*
- **Week 2:** Value-based pricing and willingness to pay. *Milestone: write one page on what your premium tier is worth to a customer, in their terms.*
- **Week 3:** How to find out what customers will pay: interviews and simple survey methods. *Milestone: run five customer conversations using a question guide.*
- **Week 4:** Tier design and the effect on existing customers. *Milestone: draft the tier structure with a migration rule.*
- **Week 5:** Competitor positioning. *Milestone: a one-page comparison.*
- **Week 6:** Build and rehearse the recommendation. *Milestone: present it to a colleague and answer their three hardest questions.*

**Ignore for now:** pricing psychology tactics, dynamic pricing, international price differences, advanced statistical methods for price research. The assistant adds that each of these is a field in its own right and none is needed for his decision.

The baseline question mattered. Lucas would have called himself "reasonably numerate" and skipped the margin arithmetic. The wrong answer showed that this was exactly where he needed to start.

He puts twelve one-and-a-half-hour sessions in his calendar, two a week, and saves the plan in a document. At the end of week 3 he reports that he managed only three customer conversations. The assistant moves two to week 4 and shortens the competitor work to make room.

## Try it on your own work

1. Choose something you need to learn for your job in the next two months.
2. Write the destination as an ability with a purpose: "so that I can…".
3. Use the prompt. Answer the baseline questions honestly, including the ones you get wrong.
4. Read the ignore list.
5. Put the sessions in your calendar and save the plan somewhere you will open again.
6. Do week one, then go back to the chat and report.

## Ignore for now

- Finding the perfect course or the ideal book before you start. A good-enough plan begun this week is worth more.
- Plans longer than eight weeks. Plan the first stretch, and plan again when you get there.

## In the module session

The instructor builds a plan live with a volunteer, shows how the baseline questions change the plan, and shows how to cut a plan to fit the time you have.
