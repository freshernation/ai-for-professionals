# Stress-testing a decision, and borrowing ideas from other fields

## Outcome

After this lesson you can use an assistant to attack your own plan before anyone else does, and to bring in ideas from fields you would never have thought to look at.

## Why it matters

By the time you have a plan, you have stopped seeing its weaknesses. The people around you often will not point them out. They are busy or polite, or they report to you. So the first serious challenge tends to come in the meeting where it matters. Meanwhile, the best idea for your problem may already exist in a field you have never worked in. An assistant can help with both. It has no stake in your plan, and it has read widely across almost every field. It will only do these jobs if you ask it in the right way.

## The idea

### Part 1: Getting real criticism

**The default is agreement**

Assistants lean towards being agreeable. Ask "Is this a good plan?" and you will mostly hear that it is, with a few mild suggestions. That does you no good, and it can do harm if it makes you more confident than you should be. You have to ask for the opposite, in plain words.

**Five ways to ask**

1. **Ask for the attack directly.**
   "Find the weaknesses in this plan. Give me the three most serious first. Don't soften them, and don't tell me what's good about it."

2. **Run a pre-mortem.**
   The psychologist Gary Klein popularised this in a 2007 *Harvard Business Review* article. You imagine the failure and work backwards from it, where the usual question would be "what might go wrong?":
   "It is a year from now and this decision has turned out badly. Write the most likely account of what went wrong."
   Assuming the failure has already happened makes it easier to name the reasons, for people and for assistants.

3. **Argue the other side.**
   "Make the strongest possible case for the option I rejected."

4. **Bring in the people affected.**
   "Respond to this as our finance director would. She cares about cost certainty and has seen two similar projects overrun." Then try it as a front-line employee, and as a customer.

5. **Hide your preference.**
   Describe two or three options neutrally and ask which is strongest, without saying which is yours. If you say "I'm leaning towards A", the answer will lean that way too.

Give the assistant the actual plan, the numbers and the constraints. Criticism of a one-line summary will be generic.

**What to do with the criticism**

- **Sort it.** Some objections will be wrong, because the assistant lacks context. Some will be minor. One or two will be real. You are looking for those.
- **Don't argue it into agreement.** If you push back, it will often give way, even when it was right. Ask yourself whether the objection is true. Whether you can talk the assistant out of it tells you nothing.
- **Fix the plan or prepare the answer.** Either change the plan, or decide how you will answer when a person raises the same point.

The decision remains yours. The assistant can show you what you missed, but it does not know your organisation and it carries none of the consequences.

### Part 2: Borrowing from other fields

**Why look outside**

Most problems are not unique. Yours has probably been faced in another industry under a different name: handing work between shifts, deciding under time pressure, keeping quality consistent across many sites. Specialists seldom find these solutions because they read within their own field. An assistant has read across all of them.

**How to ask**

- **Ask for parallels.**
  "What other fields face a problem with the same structure as this one? For each, how do they handle it?"

- **Name a distant field.**
  "How does an airline cockpit crew handle this kind of handover? What could a customer support team take from it?"

- **Use a field you know.**
  "I coach a youth football team. Explain capacity planning using a match-day squad." A subject you know lets you grasp one you don't.

- **Push for surprise.**
  "Give me three analogies from fields as far from mine as possible. Then tell me where each analogy breaks down."

The final clause matters. An analogy gives you ideas to test, and it proves nothing. Always ask where the comparison stops working.

## Worked example

Aisha is head of customer service at an online furniture retailer. Weekend contacts have grown and her team is stretched. Her plan is to outsource weekend support to an agency from next quarter. She is fairly sure it is right, and she presents it on Thursday.

**The pre-mortem**

She gives the assistant the plan: volumes, the agency's quote, the current weekend rota, satisfaction scores and the two alternatives she rejected. Then:

```
It is a year from now and outsourcing weekend support has turned out
to be a mistake. Write the most likely account of what went wrong.
Be specific to the details I've given. Then list the three most
serious risks in order.
```

The account it writes is uncomfortable to read. Agency staff could answer simple queries, but a third of weekend contacts are about damaged deliveries. These need access to the warehouse system and the authority to approve a replacement, and the plan gave the agency neither. So those cases waited for Monday, weekend satisfaction fell, and Monday became the in-house team's worst day.

Aisha checks her data. Damaged-delivery contacts are 31% of weekend volume. She had been thinking about total volume and had not looked at what the contacts were about.

**The other side**

```
Make the strongest case for the option I rejected: a paid voluntary
weekend rota for our own staff.
```

The case is better than she expected. Her own staff already have the access and authority, the cost difference is smaller than she had assumed once agency onboarding is counted, and several team members have asked for extra hours.

**A different field**

She still needs a way to handle what cannot be resolved at the weekend.

```
What other fields have to hand unresolved cases from one team to the
next without losing information or making the person repeat
themselves? How do they do it, and where does each comparison with a
customer service team break down?
```

The assistant describes hospital shift handovers, where a short fixed structure is used for every patient: the situation, the background, the assessment and what is needed next. It notes where the comparison is weak. Hospital staff hand over face to face and share one record, while her teams would not overlap. It suggests that the fixed structure can still be used as a written note on each open case.

On Thursday Aisha presents a changed plan. The agency handles simple weekend queries. Two in-house staff on a paid voluntary rota handle damaged deliveries with full authority. Every case left open gets a four-line handover note. When the operations director asks, "What about the cases the agency can't resolve?", she already has the answer.

## Try it on your own work

1. Take a decision or plan you are currently fairly confident about.
2. Give the assistant the real details, then run a pre-mortem.
3. Ask for the strongest case for the option you rejected.
4. Sort the objections: wrong, minor, real. Check one "real" one against your own data.
5. Ask which other fields face the same structure of problem, and where each comparison breaks down.
6. Change one thing in your plan, or prepare one answer.

## Ignore for now

- Formal decision-analysis methods and scoring matrices.
- Asking the assistant to make the decision. It doesn't have your context and won't bear the consequences.
## In the module session

The instructor stress-tests a real plan from the group using all five approaches, shows how easily an assistant can be argued out of a correct objection, and runs a cross-field exercise on a problem the group shares.
