# From blank page to first draft

## Outcome

After this lesson you can get from scattered thoughts to a solid first draft in minutes, with the ideas still your own.

## Why it matters

For most people the hardest part of writing is starting. You know roughly what you want to say, but the blank page asks you to find the structure, the opening and the right words all at once. So the report gets put off, or it gets written badly at the last minute. An assistant removes that obstacle. Used carelessly, it also removes the thinking, and you end up with a smooth document that says nothing in particular. This lesson shows how to get the speed and keep the thinking.

## The idea

**Separate the thinking from the wording**

Writing involves two jobs: deciding what to say, and finding the words to say it. The first is yours. Only you know what happened, what you concluded and what you recommend. The second is where the assistant is quick. If you hand over both, the assistant will invent content to fill the space, and you get generic text. If you supply the thinking and delegate the wording, you get your own document, written faster.

So the rule for a first draft is that the points come from you.

**Three ways to start**

*1. Dump, then organise.*
Get everything out of your head without worrying about order or wording. Type it as rough notes, or better, dictate it. Most assistants accept voice input, and people say far more when they talk than when they type. Ramble for two minutes about what happened, what you think and what you are unsure of. Then:

```
Here are my rough notes for [what you're writing], for [reader].
Organise them into an outline. Use only what I've said. Don't add
points of your own. If something important seems to be missing, list
it as a question at the end.
```

*2. Outline first, then draft.*
Do not ask for the full text straight away. Ask for the structure, look at it, and change it. Moving a section in an outline takes five seconds. Moving it in a finished draft means a rewrite.

```
Suggest two different ways to structure this, as headings with one
line each on what goes underneath. Don't write the text yet.
```

Asking for two structures shows you a choice you would not have known you had. Pick one, adjust it, then ask for the draft.

*3. Be interviewed.*
If you are not sure what you want to say, have the assistant draw it out: "Ask me questions, one at a time, until you have enough to draft this." Your answers become the raw material.

**Keeping the draft honest**

Three instructions prevent most problems:

- **"Use only what I've given you."** Without this, the assistant pads with plausible detail.
- **"Mark anything you had to assume or that is missing with [square brackets]."** Gaps then show up as gaps, where they would otherwise be filled with invention. A draft that says "[cost figure needed]" is more useful than one with a made-up number.
- **State the reader and the purpose.** Who is it for, and what should they do or know afterwards?

**Draft in pieces when it is long**

For anything over a page, draft one section at a time. You can correct the direction early, and the quality stays higher than when everything is produced in one go.

**A first draft is supposed to be rough**

The purpose of a first draft is to exist, so that you have something to react to. Expect to change it. Editing a mediocre draft is far easier than writing a good one from nothing, and that is the whole advantage. Read it, mark what is wrong, and improve it. The draft belongs to you once you have done that work on it.

**What you still have to do**

- Check every fact, name, number and date against what you know.
- Make sure it says what you actually think. Assistants soften conclusions and add balance you may not intend.
- Rewrite the parts that matter most in your own words: the opening, the recommendation, anything personal.

## Worked example

Tunde is a software engineer. Last week a faulty update took his company's ordering system offline for three hours. He has to write a one-page summary for the leadership team, who are not technical. He has the incident timeline, the chat logs, and strong views about what should change. He has been putting off writing it for two days.

**Dump.** He opens his assistant and dictates for two minutes: what broke, when it was noticed, why it took so long to fix, the fact that the test environment does not match the live one, his view that this was an accident waiting to happen, and three things he wants done about it. It comes out in no particular order.

**Outline.** He adds:

```
These are my rough notes for a one-page incident summary for our
leadership team. They are not technical and want to know what
happened, what it cost, and what we're doing about it.

Suggest two different ways to structure this, as headings with one
line each. Use only what I've said. Don't write the text yet. List
anything that seems to be missing as a question.
```

The assistant offers two structures. One is chronological: what happened, why, what next. The other leads with the conclusion: the cause and the fix in the first paragraph, then the timeline, then the three changes with their cost. It also asks two questions: how many orders were affected, and who will own each change?

Tunde had not planned to include either. He realises that leadership will ask both. He chooses the second structure, because his readers want the answer first.

**Draft.** 

```
Use the second structure. Draft it in plain language with no
technical terms. If one is unavoidable, explain it in a few words.
Under 350 words. Use only my notes. Put [square brackets] around
anything missing, including the order numbers and the owners.
```

The draft comes back in about ten seconds. The opening reads: "On Tuesday a software update took our ordering system offline for three hours. The update had passed our tests, but our test system is set up differently from the live one, so the fault did not show until customers were affected." It has bracketed gaps for the number of orders and for each owner.

**Check and own.** Tunde reads it against the timeline. One sentence says the problem was "identified within minutes". His notes say forty minutes, and he corrects it. The recommendation has been softened to "we may wish to consider aligning the test environment". He rewrites it in his own words: "We need to make the test system match the live one. Until we do, this will happen again." He gets the order figure from the sales team and fills in the owners.

Twenty-five minutes after he started, the summary is done. The assistant gave him the structure and the plain wording. The cause, the judgement and the recommendation were his.

## Try it on your own work

1. Pick something you have been putting off writing.
2. Dump your thoughts: type rough notes, or dictate for two minutes.
3. Ask for two possible structures, using only what you said, with missing items listed as questions.
4. Choose and adjust a structure. Answer the questions.
5. Ask for the draft, with brackets around anything assumed or missing.
6. Check every fact. Rewrite the opening and the main conclusion in your own words.

## Ignore for now

- Getting the wording right on the first pass. That is for editing.
- Elaborate templates for every document type.
- Asking the assistant for ideas on what your view should be. Bring the view, and let it help with the words.
