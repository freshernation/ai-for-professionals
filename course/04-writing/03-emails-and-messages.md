# Emails and messages

## Outcome

After this lesson you can use an assistant to write clearer emails faster, handle the difficult ones with more care, and catch a tone problem before you press send.

## Why it matters

Email and chat messages take up more of the working day than any other kind of writing, and they are where most people first try AI. The time saved on each message is small, but there are dozens a day. The larger benefit is on the hard ones: the message you have rewritten four times, or the reply you are too annoyed to write well. A draft and a second opinion help most there. Email is also where a careless AI message does visible damage, because the person you sent it to reads every word.

## The idea

**Four jobs the assistant does well**

*1. Drafting from your points.*
You know what you want to say. Give the points and let the assistant write it out.

```
Draft a reply to the email below.
What I want to say: yes to the meeting, but not before the 20th;
I need the revised figures two days beforehand; Sam should attend.
Tone: friendly, brief. Under 80 words.

[email pasted]
```

A line or two of points is enough. Without them the assistant has to guess your answer, and it will guess politely and wrongly.

*2. Catching up on a long thread.*
Paste in a long chain and ask: "Summarise this thread: what has been decided, what is still open, and what is being asked of me." Then draft your reply from the summary. This turns twenty minutes of scrolling into two. Check the summary against the thread for anything important.

*3. Shortening and sharpening.*
Most emails are too long and hide the request. Paste yours in and ask: "Cut this by half. Put what I need from the reader in the first two lines." A message that states its request up front gets answered sooner.

*4. Checking the tone.*
Before sending something sensitive, ask how it will be read:

```
I'm about to send this to [who, and the situation]. How is it likely
to come across? Is anything unclear, or could anything be read as
sharper, colder or more defensive than I intend? Don't rewrite it.
Just tell me.
```

This is one of the most useful things an assistant does. Tone is hard to judge in your own writing, especially when you are irritated or anxious.

**What a good work email looks like**

Give the assistant these as standing rules, or check drafts against them:

- **The point or request comes first.** The reader should know why you are writing by the end of the second line.
- **One message, one purpose.** Two unrelated requests should be two emails.
- **Say exactly what you need and by when.** "Could you confirm by Thursday?" gets a reply. "Let me know your thoughts" often doesn't.
- **Short.** Most replies can be under 100 words.
- **Sounds like you.** The reader knows how you write.

Chat messages follow the same rules and are shorter and plainer. Tell the assistant which it is. A message written like an email looks odd in a chat tool.

**The difficult ones**

For a message that carries bad news, a disagreement or an apology:

1. **Write down what must be said**, bluntly, for yourself only. This is the substance.
2. **Give the assistant the substance and the situation**: who the person is, your relationship with them, what you want to be true afterwards.
3. **Ask for a draft**, then run the tone check on it.
4. **Rewrite the key sentences yourself.** People can tell when a hard message was written by a machine, and at a sensitive moment that reads as not caring. The structure can come from the assistant, but the words that matter should come from you.

If you are angry, let the assistant help you write the calm version. Don't send the first one. Wait an hour if you can.

**Before you press send**

- **You send it. The assistant doesn't.** Read every message in full.
- **Check the details:** names, dates, times, amounts, and attachments you mention.
- **Check for promises.** Assistants add helpful commitments you never made, such as "I'll have this with you by Friday" or "happy to arrange a call".
- **Cut the filler.** Delete "I hope this email finds you well" and anything else you would never say.
- **Mind what you paste in.** Emails are full of names and confidential details. Apply your usual rules about what may go into which tool.

**Reusable messages**

For messages you send often, such as a meeting follow-up, a polite chase or a holding reply, build a short prompt with brackets for the details, and keep it. Ten seconds per message adds up.

## Worked example

Grace is an HR business partner. An internal candidate, Ben, applied for a team lead role and did not get it. The role went to someone from outside. Ben has been with the company for six years and is well regarded, and Grace wants to keep him. She needs to send a message asking him to meet. She knows he will guess the outcome from it, so it has to be right.

She begins with the substance, for herself: *he didn't get it; the external candidate had direct people-management experience, which he lacks; he interviewed well on technical depth; we want to keep him; there is a real development route.*

Then, with the name removed and the details kept general:

```
Help me draft a short message to an internal candidate who applied
for a team lead role and was not selected. He has been here six
years, is highly valued, and will be disappointed. The message is to
ask him to meet me tomorrow so I can tell him in person and discuss
next steps.

It should be honest that the decision has been made and that it isn't
the outcome he hoped for. I don't want him waiting overnight to find
out, and I don't want to deliver the full news in writing. Warm,
direct, no corporate language. Under 70 words.
```

The draft:

> Hi [Name], I wanted to let you know that a decision has been made on the team lead role. Unfortunately it isn't the outcome you were hoping for, and I'd like to talk it through with you properly rather than over message. Could we meet tomorrow at 10? I also want to discuss what comes next for you here, as we really value your contribution.

Grace runs the tone check: "How is this likely to come across to someone who is disappointed?"

The assistant's reply is useful. It says the message is clear and kind. It adds that "we really value your contribution" is a stock phrase which, placed straight after bad news, may read as a consolation line and not as sincere. It also points out that "Unfortunately" is doing a job she could do more directly.

She agrees. She rewrites the middle and the end in her own words:

> Hi Ben, a decision has been made on the team lead role, and I'm sorry to say it isn't the one you were hoping for. I'd rather talk it through with you properly than do it by message. Could we meet tomorrow at 10? I have some specific thoughts on what comes next for you, and I'd like to hear yours.

She checks the time against her calendar and sends it. The assistant gave her a structure and noticed a hollow phrase. The sentences Ben will remember are the ones she wrote herself.

## In your tool

- **Gemini** is built into Gmail for people on eligible Google plans, with a "Help me write" option in the compose window.
- **Microsoft Copilot** drafts and summarises inside Outlook for users with a Copilot licence.
- **ChatGPT and Claude** work by pasting text in, or through a connection to your mailbox where your plan and your employer allow it.

An assistant inside your mail client saves copying and pasting. The same rules apply: give it your points, and read before sending.

## Try it on your own work

1. Take a reply you need to write today. Give the assistant the email and your points in one line. Edit the draft and send it.
2. Find a long thread in your inbox. Ask for a summary: decided, open, and asked of you. Check it against the thread.
3. Take an email you have already written and ask for it at half the length, with the request first.
4. Choose a sensitive message, past or pending, and run the tone check.
5. Pick one kind of message you send every week and save a prompt for it.

## Ignore for now

- Fully automatic replies. A person should read everything that goes out under your name.
- Tools that draft replies to every incoming message unasked.
- Long email templates. Short points and a clear tone instruction do more.
