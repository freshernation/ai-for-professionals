# Slides and presentations, in your own voice

## Outcome

After this lesson you can use an assistant to build the argument of a presentation, turn it into clear slides, rehearse the questions, and make sure the result sounds like you and not like a machine.

## Why it matters

Presentations go wrong in two familiar ways. Either the slides are the speaker's notes, with dense text that the audience reads while ignoring the speaker, or they are a pile of content with no argument running through it. AI makes both easier to do, since a tool can now produce thirty slides in a minute. It can also help you avoid both, if you use it on the thinking before the slides. A presentation is also personal in a way a report is not. You stand up and say it. If the words are not yours, the audience can tell.

## The idea

### Part 1: Story first, slides second

**Start with one sentence**

Before opening any slide tool, finish this: *"After this presentation, the audience should [know / decide / do] …"*. If you cannot, more slides will not help. Ask the assistant to help you get there: "Here is what I want to cover. What is the single message? Give me three candidate versions."

**Build the storyline as headlines**

A good deck can be understood by reading the slide titles alone, in order. So write the titles first, as full sentences that each make a point:

- Weak title: "Q3 Sales"
- Strong title: "Q3 sales grew 8%, entirely from existing customers"

```
I'm presenting to [audience] for [time]. The one message is: [message].
Here is my material: [notes, figures].

Write a storyline as slide titles only. Each title must be a full
sentence that makes a point. Read in order, the titles alone should
make the argument. No more than [number] slides. Use only my material
and mark any gap with [brackets].
```

Read the titles aloud. If the argument does not flow, fix it now, while all it costs is moving lines of text.

**One point per slide**

Each slide exists to support its title. That means a chart, a few words or an image, and nothing else. The detail belongs in what you say. Ask the assistant: "For each title, what is the one piece of evidence that best supports it? Suggest the form: chart, single number, quote or picture."

**Speaker notes**

Ask for notes for each slide in short spoken sentences. Then rewrite them as you would say them (see Part 2).

**Cut**

Nearly every presentation is too long. Ask: "I have ten minutes, not twenty. Which slides go, and what do I lose?"

**Generating the slides themselves**

Several tools can now create an actual deck from your outline. Treat the result as a first layout. Check every number and chart against your source, since charts drawn by AI can show the wrong values. Remove surplus text, and apply your organisation's template. The storyline is the valuable part, and the deck gives it a form.

**Rehearse the questions**

```
I'm presenting this to [audience]. Play the most sceptical person in
the room. Ask me the hardest questions, one at a time, and tell me
where my answer was weak.
```

Ten minutes of this is the best preparation there is. You can do it in voice mode on the way to the meeting.

### Part 2: Making it sound like you

**The AI accent**

Unedited AI writing has recognisable habits, and audiences increasingly notice them:

- Grand openers: "In today's rapidly evolving landscape…"
- Everything in threes: "faster, smarter and more efficient".
- The balanced contrast: "It's not just about X; it's about Y."
- A tidy summarising line at the end of every paragraph.
- Fashionable abstract words where a plain one would do.
- An even, pleasant, slightly enthusiastic tone throughout.

No single one of these is wrong. Together they tell the listener that nobody in particular wrote this. In an email that weakens your credibility. Spoken aloud by you, it sounds false.

**Teach it your voice**

Show the assistant how you actually speak and write.

1. Gather three or four samples of your own: emails you wrote, notes from a talk you gave, a transcript of yourself speaking. Use material you wrote without AI.
2. Ask: "Describe how I write and speak, in ten specific observations: sentence length, how I open, words I use, what I avoid."
3. Correct the description where it is wrong. Save it.
4. Use it: "Write the speaker notes in this style."

This will get you close, though not all the way.

**Then make it yours**

- **Say it aloud.** Read the notes out loud. Wherever you stumble, or would never say the phrase, change it. This is the best test there is.
- **Add what only you have.** A story from your own experience, an opinion, a moment of doubt, a joke that suits you. An assistant cannot generate these, and they are what audiences remember.
- **Take out the accent.** Delete the grand opener, break up the threes, cut the summarising line.
- **Keep your plain words.** If you would say "it didn't work", do not present "the initiative fell short of expectations".

The assistant gives you structure and a draft. The voice has to come from your own editing, and it is worth the fifteen minutes.

## Worked example

Beatriz is finance director of a group of veterinary clinics. On Thursday she presents third-quarter results to the board: fifteen minutes, eight people, and two of them new. Her usual deck has twenty-two slides of tables.

**One message.** She gives the assistant her figures and notes and asks for candidate messages. She picks one and sharpens it: *"Revenue is on plan, but margin is being squeezed by locum costs, and I need the board to approve a recruitment budget to fix it."*

**Storyline.** She asks for titles only, with a maximum of eight slides. The assistant returns:

1. Q3 revenue was 4.2m, 2% ahead of plan
2. Operating margin fell from 14% to 11%
3. Three quarters of the fall is locum cover for unfilled vet posts
4. We have had nine vet vacancies open for more than four months
5. A locum costs us roughly twice a salaried vet per day
6. Filling six posts would recover about one and a half points of margin
7. We propose a recruitment budget of 240,000 for the next two quarters
8. The decision needed today

She reads them aloud. The argument holds. She moves slide 8's request up to follow slide 1, so that the board knows from the start what they are being asked for. She checks each number against her own management accounts. Slide 6 first said "about three points". Her own calculation gives one and a half, and she corrects it.

**Evidence.** For each title she asks for the one supporting item. The suggestions are a single large number for slide 1, a two-bar chart for slide 2, a simple breakdown for slide 3, and so on. The twenty-two tables go into an appendix.

**Voice.** The assistant's speaker notes for slide 2 begin: "Turning to profitability, we see a more nuanced picture emerging." Beatriz would never say that. She pastes in the notes from her last two board presentations, asks for a description of her style, and gets ten observations, among them: "Opens with the number. Short sentences. States bad news plainly, without softening. Rarely uses adjectives." She agrees with nine of the ten and asks for the notes again in that style.

Slide 2 now reads: "Margin fell to 11%. Last year it was 14. That's the problem I want to spend most of today on." She reads all eight aloud, changes four phrases, and adds a story of her own to slide 4, about a clinic manager who told her she had spent more on locums in August than on anything except rent.

**Rehearsal.** She runs the sceptic prompt. The third question is: "Why will recruitment work now when these posts have been open for four months?" Her first answer is weak, and the assistant says so. She prepares a better one, with the specifics of the recruitment plan, and adds a backup slide.

On Thursday a new board member asks that question, almost word for word.

## In your tool

- **Gemini** can create presentations inside Google Slides on eligible paid and Workspace plans.
- **Microsoft Copilot** creates and edits decks inside PowerPoint for users with a Copilot licence.
- **ChatGPT and Claude** can each produce a PowerPoint file from a chat, and each offers an add-in that works inside PowerPoint.

Availability depends on your plan and changes often. All of them work better from a storyline you have already agreed than from a bare topic.

## Try it on your own work

1. Take a presentation you have coming up. Write the one-sentence message.
2. Ask for the storyline as sentence titles only. Read them aloud and fix the flow.
3. Check every number in the titles against your source.
4. Gather three samples of your own writing or speaking. Get a description of your style, and correct it.
5. Get speaker notes in that style. Read them aloud and change what you would not say. Add one story of your own.
6. Rehearse with the sceptic prompt for ten minutes.

## Ignore for now

- Design, animation and image generation for slides. The argument comes first.
- Letting a tool generate the deck from a topic alone.
- Trying to remove every trace of AI help. The aim is that it sounds like you and is true.

## In the module session

The instructor builds a storyline live from a volunteer's material, extracts a learner's voice from samples and shows the before and after, and runs a rehearsal with the assistant as the sceptic.
