# Editing: asking for criticism, not praise

## Outcome

After this lesson you can use an assistant as a tough editor that finds the weak arguments, the repetition and the vague language in your writing, while leaving the writing yours.

## Why it matters

Good writers have editors. Most people at work do not. They finish a draft, read it once, and send it. The weak point in the argument goes unnoticed until the reader finds it. An assistant can be the editor you never had, and it is available at the moment you finish a draft. It will only do the job if you ask in the right way. By default it tells you your draft is strong and offers to polish it.

## The idea

**Why the default is useless**

Paste in a draft and ask "What do you think?", and you will usually get three compliments and a couple of gentle suggestions. Ask it to "improve this", and it rewrites the whole thing in its own smooth, even voice. Your argument is no stronger, and the result no longer sounds like you. Neither of these is editing.

An editor's job is to make your piece better by showing you what is wrong with it. So ask for the problems.

**The editor prompt**

```
Act as a demanding editor. The reader is [who], and the purpose is
[what it should achieve].

Don't rewrite it and don't tell me what's good. List the problems,
most serious first:
1. Where is the argument weak, unsupported or unclear?
2. What is repeated?
3. Which sentences are vague where they should be specific?
4. What could be cut with nothing lost?
5. Is the structure right for this reader?

Quote the exact words each time.
```

Three parts of that prompt matter most:

- **"Don't rewrite it."** You want a diagnosis first. You decide what to change.
- **"Don't tell me what's good."** This removes the padding and the flattery.
- **"Quote the exact words."** This forces specific criticism. "Some sections could be tighter" is no help. "Paragraph 3 makes the same point as paragraph 1" is something you can act on.

**Three things to hunt for**

*Weak arguments.* A claim with no evidence. A conclusion that does not follow. An obvious objection left unanswered. Ask: "What would a sceptical reader challenge first?"

*Repetition.* Most drafts say their main point three times in slightly different words. Ask: "Where have I said the same thing more than once?"

*Vague language.* "Significant improvement." "A number of issues." "In due course." Each of these stands in for a fact the writer did not go and find. Ask: "Which phrases are vague, and what specific information should replace each?" The assistant cannot supply the fact. It can show you where one is missing.

**Edit in passes**

Fix the big things before the small ones. There is no point polishing a sentence in a paragraph you are about to delete.

1. **Argument and content.** Is it right? Is it complete? Is anything unsupported?
2. **Structure.** Is it in the best order for this reader? Is the main point early enough?
3. **Sentences.** Length, clarity, repetition, vague words.

Ask for one pass at a time.

**Read it as someone else**

Tell the assistant who is reading: "Read this as our finance director, who is short of time and has turned down two similar requests." A named, particular reader produces sharper criticism than a general one, because the assistant can ask what that person would object to.

**You make the changes**

When the list comes back, sort it. Some points will be right, some will be wrong because the assistant lacks context, and some will come down to taste. Take the ones that are right and fix them yourself, in your own words. That is what keeps the piece yours.

If you do want help with wording, keep the request narrow:

- "Suggest two ways to rewrite this one sentence."
- "Cut this paragraph by a third without losing any point."
- "Change as little as possible. Fix only what is unclear."

Do not ask for a rewrite of the whole piece. You would be swapping your voice for a generic one.

**It always finds something**

Ask for five problems and you will get five, even if the draft is excellent. A long list is not proof that the draft is bad. After a round or two the criticisms become matters of preference. That is when you stop.

## Worked example

Elena is a marketing manager at a company that sells inventory software to independent pharmacies. She has written a customer case study and thinks it is nearly ready. Before sending it to the customer for approval, she asks for an edit.

Her first try is "What do you think of this case study?". She is told it is "engaging and well structured, with a compelling narrative", and offered two small tweaks.

She tries again with the editor prompt, adding: "The reader is a pharmacy owner deciding whether to book a demo. The purpose is to make them believe this would work in their shop."

The reply is very different:

> 1. **The result is buried.** The strongest fact, "stock write-offs fell from 4.1% to 1.6% of purchases", first appears in paragraph five. A pharmacy owner skimming this will not get that far. The opening two paragraphs describe the customer's history instead.
>
> 2. **An unsupported claim.** "Staff now spend far less time on stock-taking." How much less? Without a figure, a sceptical reader will discount it.
>
> 3. **Repetition.** That the software is "easy to use" is stated in paragraph 2 ("intuitive interface"), paragraph 4 ("simple to learn") and paragraph 6 ("user-friendly"). Once, with evidence, would be stronger.
>
> 4. **Vague language.** "Significant savings", "a number of challenges", "seamless integration". Each needs a specific or should go.
>
> 5. **A missing objection.** A pharmacy owner's first worry is disruption during the changeover. The case study does not mention how long setup took or whether the shop stayed open.

Elena goes through the list. Points 1, 3 and 4 are plainly right. For point 2 she has no figure, so she emails the customer. The answer is that the monthly stock-take went from a full day to about two hours, and that goes in. Point 5 is something she had not thought of, and it is the most valuable item on the list. The customer says setup took one weekend and the shop did not close. She adds two sentences.

She restructures the piece herself, opening with the write-off figure and the stock-take time. She cuts two of the three "easy to use" mentions and replaces "significant savings" with the actual number. For one clumsy sentence she asks for two alternatives and takes neither, but they show her a third way to put it.

Finally she asks for a sentence-level pass: "Now list any sentence over 30 words and anything a pharmacy owner would find jargon." Three sentences get shortened.

The case study still sounds like Elena. It now opens with the fact that matters, supports its claims with figures, and answers the question a buyer would ask.

## Try it on your own work

1. Take a draft you consider finished or nearly finished.
2. Ask "What do you think?" and note how much of the reply is praise.
3. Use the editor prompt, naming the real reader and purpose.
4. Sort the list into right, wrong and taste.
5. Fix the "right" items yourself. If a vague phrase needs a fact, go and find the fact.
6. Run one sentence-level pass. Then stop.

## Ignore for now

- Grammar and spelling tools. Your word processor already has them, and they matter least.
- Readability scores.
- Having the assistant rewrite the piece "in a more professional tone".
