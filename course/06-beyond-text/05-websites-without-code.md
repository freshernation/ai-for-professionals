# Websites and landing pages without code

## Outcome

After this lesson you can describe a simple web page, have an assistant build it, replace anything invented with real content, and get it published properly.

## Why it matters

Sooner or later most professionals need a page: sign-up for an event, a project summary, a short explainer for a new service. Now you can describe the page and see it working in a minute. The page that comes back looks professional, and that is where the risk lies. A polished layout can carry invented statistics and made-up testimonials. It can include a form that sends people's details nowhere, or somewhere it should not. A page speaks for your organisation in public, so most of the work is in what you put on it and how you publish it.

## The idea

### What this is good for

- **Landing pages** with one purpose: register, download, get in touch.
- **Event pages.**
- **Internal pages:** a project summary or a how-to.
- **Prototypes:** a realistic mock-up to show a web team or an agency exactly what you want.

It is not the way to build an online shop, anything with user accounts, or a large site that many people will maintain.

### Describing a page

A good page does one job. Decide what it is before you ask for anything.

1. **Purpose and the one action.** What should a visitor do? Register, download or email you. Choose one.
2. **Audience.** Who is arriving, what do they already know, and what would make them hesitate?
3. **Sections, in order.** Usually: a headline and one line of explanation; the key facts; the reasons to act; answers to likely questions; the action again.
4. **The content.** Your real words and facts. The assistant will not invent them, or will invent them badly.
5. **Look and tone.** Your organisation's colours and style, plain or lively.

```
Build a single landing page for [event/offer]. The one action is
[action].

Audience: [who, what they know, what might put them off].
Sections in this order: [list].
Use exactly this content: [your text, dates, facts].
Style: clean and simple, colours [colours], plenty of white space.
It must work well on a phone.

Do not invent any statistics, testimonials, logos, names or quotes.
Where you would want one and I haven't given it, put a clearly marked
placeholder in [square brackets].
```

The last paragraph is essential. Left alone, an assistant fills a landing page with what landing pages usually have: "Trusted by 500+ companies", a five-star quote from "Sarah M., Operations Director", a row of well-known logos. All of it is invented. If published, these are false claims made in your organisation's name.

Then improve it one change at a time, as you would any draft.

### Before anyone sees it

**1. Every fact.** Dates, times, places, prices and names. Read each one against your source.

**2. Nothing invented.** Search the page for numbers, quotations, names and logos. Each must be real and yours to use. Remove every placeholder or fill it with something true.

**3. Images.** They must be ones you may use, and must not present invented people as your staff or customers.

**4. The form.** If the page collects anything from visitors, including a name and email address, you need answers to three questions. Where do submissions go? Who can see them? What have you told people about how their details will be used? A form built by an assistant may store entries where you cannot find them, or may not work at all. The simplest route is usually to link the page's button to your organisation's approved form or registration tool.

**5. On a phone.** Most visitors will use one. Open the page on yours.

**6. Accessibility.** Text should contrast clearly with its background, and images should have descriptions. Ask the assistant to check the page for accessibility problems and fix them. Then look yourself.

**7. Links and buttons.** Click every one.

### Getting it published

You have built a page. Publishing it is a separate decision.

- **A share link from your assistant.** Good for showing colleagues a draft. The address will not be your organisation's, anyone with the link can usually open it, and it may not stay available.
- **A website-building service.** Many now build from a description and include hosting, your own address and working forms.
- **Your organisation's website.** For anything published in your employer's name, this is the right place. Give your web or communications team the working page as the brief.

For anything public in your organisation's name, expect requirements on brand, on privacy (a notice saying what you will do with people's details), on approval and on security. Ask about them early. A working prototype makes the conversation with the web team fast, because they can see exactly what you want.

## Worked example

Anya is an engineering manager at a firm that designs water treatment plants. The firm is holding an open evening for final-year engineering students, to recruit graduates, and she has been asked to organise it. She needs a page where students can find the details and register. The communications team is three weeks behind, so she decides to bring them a finished prototype.

**The description.** She writes out the real content first: date, time, address, the three short talks and who is giving them, what is provided, and who to contact. Then she uses the prompt above. The one action is "Register for the open evening". The audience is "final-year engineering students who have probably not heard of us and are comparing several employers". She gives the firm's two brand colours.

**The first version.** It appears in half a minute and looks very good.

She reads it closely.

- Under the headline is a line of text: "Join 2,000+ engineers shaping the future of water." She had not written that. The firm employs 340 people.
- Lower down is a quotation: "The open evening changed my career. — Priya S., Graduate Engineer, 2024." No such person exists. The assistant had not used brackets for it, despite the instruction.
- A row of placeholders reads "[Partner university logos]".
- The register button opens a form that asks for name, email, university and dietary requirements.

**Removing what is invented.** She has the "2,000+" line replaced with a true one: "340 engineers. 60 treatment plants across four countries." She has checked both figures. She replaces the quotation with two sentences from a real graduate, who agrees to be named. She has the logo row deleted, since the firm has no agreement to use any university's logo.

She then asks: "List every statistic, quotation, name and claim on this page, and tell me which came from my content and which you added." The list shows one more addition, "award-winning graduate programme". There is no award. It goes.

**The form.** She asks the assistant where submissions would go. The answer is that the form is for display only and stores nothing. Thirty students could have "registered" and no record would exist.

Dietary requirements can also reveal health or religious information, and that calls for care. She does not try to make the form work. She has the button changed to link to the firm's approved event registration tool, which the HR team already uses, and she sets up the event there with a privacy notice written by the firm.

**Checks.** On her phone, the list of talks runs off the edge of the screen, and she has it fixed. The accessibility check reports that pale green text on white is hard to read and that two images have no description. She clicks every link. The map link points to the wrong building, so she corrects it.

**Publishing.** She sends the share link to the communications team with a note: "This is a working prototype of the open evening page. All content is checked. The button goes to our registration tool. Could this go on the careers section?"

Because nothing needs to be designed or written, they rebuild it in the company's system in an hour. They replace her AI-generated header image with a photograph of one of the firm's real plants, and add the standard footer with the privacy link.

The page is live on the firm's own website two days later. The assistant's first version had contained three invented claims and a form that did nothing, and it had looked finished.

## In your tool

- **ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini** can each build a web page from a description and show it working beside the chat. Each can share it by a link.
- **Website-building services** with AI built in add hosting, your own address and working forms.

## Try it on your own work

1. Think of a page that would help with something you are working on: an event, a project summary, a resource list.
2. Decide its purpose, its audience and its one action. Write the real content first.
3. Use the prompt, including the instruction not to invent anything.
4. Ask for a list of every statistic, quotation, name and claim, and where each came from. Remove or replace anything you did not supply.
5. Check it on your phone, ask for an accessibility check, and click every link.
6. If it collects anything from visitors, find out where the data would go. Then decide where the page should be published, and who needs to approve it.

## Ignore for now

- Search engine optimisation and analytics.
- Multi-page sites. One page that does one job is the skill to learn first.
