# Images

## Outcome

After this lesson you can describe an image well enough to get what you need, improve it in a few steps, and decide whether you may use it and how to label it.

## Why it matters

Most professionals need an image now and then and cannot draw. The old choices were stock photographs that never quite fit, or a wait for someone with design skills. Now you can describe the image and have it in a minute. That ease brings three questions the old choices never raised: whether the image is accurate, whether you are allowed to use it, and whether someone could mistake it for something real. This lesson covers all of them.

## The idea

### What image generation is good for at work

- **Illustrations** for slides, documents and training material.
- **Concept sketches and mock-ups**: "roughly like this", to brief a designer or test an idea.
- **Headers and backgrounds** for invitations, newsletters and internal pages.
- **Editing an existing image**: removing a background, extending a picture, changing a detail.
- **Simple icons and diagrams** in a consistent style.

It is a poor choice for:

- **Charts of real data.** An image generator draws something that looks like a chart. Its bars do not match your numbers. Make charts from the data.
- **Accurate technical diagrams, maps and floor plans.** These will look plausible and be wrong in the details.
- **Your logo and exact brand assets.** Use the real files.
- **Pictures of real people, real places or real events.** See the rights section below.

### Describing an image

A bare request gives a generic picture, just as it does with text. Cover five things:

1. **Subject.** What is in the picture, specifically.
2. **Setting and mood.** Where, what time, what feeling.
3. **Style.** A flat illustration, a line drawing, a realistic photograph, a watercolour. This choice has the largest effect on the result.
4. **Composition.** What is in the foreground, where there should be empty space for text, and the angle.
5. **Format.** Wide for a slide or header, square for a post, tall for a poster.

Add what you do not want: "no text in the image", "no people's faces".

```
A flat, simple illustration of a refrigerated delivery van parked at
the loading bay of a small restaurant at dawn. Calm, reliable mood.
Muted blues and warm orange light. The van is on the left third;
leave the right two-thirds as open sky for a headline. Wide format,
16:9. No text, no logos, no people.
```

### Improving it

Do not start again each time. Edit what you have:

- "Keep everything the same, but make the van white."
- "Same image, less detail in the background."
- "Give me three variations of the colour scheme."

Change one thing per step. If the picture drifts from what you liked, go back to the earlier version and try a different instruction.

### What to check

- **Text inside the image.** Tools have improved a great deal, and misspelled or garbled words still appear. Read every word. For anything important, generate the image without text and add the words yourself in your slide or design tool.
- **Details.** Hands, the number of fingers, objects merging into each other, and signs and labels in the background.
- **Accuracy.** If the picture shows a process, a product or a piece of equipment, check that it is right. A safety poster showing the wrong way to lift is worse than no poster.

### Rights and disclosure, plainly

This area is unsettled and differs between countries. None of what follows is legal advice. Your organisation's policy comes first. These are the points every user should know.

**1. You may not own what you generate.**
In several countries, copyright protects work made by a human author. The United States Copyright Office said in January 2025 that writing a prompt is not, on its own, enough to make you the author of the image that results. In practice this means that you may be unable to stop others from copying a purely AI-generated image. For a slide, that rarely matters. For a logo or a product design, it matters a great deal. Do not use raw AI output for anything you need to own.

**2. It may resemble something that belongs to someone else.**
A generated image can come out close to an existing artwork, character or brand. Reduce the risk:
- do not ask for the style of a named living artist;
- do not ask for well-known characters, brands or logos;
- if a result looks familiar, do not use it.

**3. Read the tool's terms for commercial use.**
Most paid tools allow commercial use of what you make. Free tiers sometimes do not. If the image is going on anything public, check.

**4. Do not create images of real people.**
Do not generate a picture of a real, identifiable person without their agreement. That includes colleagues, customers and public figures. Do not upload someone's photograph to alter it without their consent.

**5. Label what could be mistaken for real.**
An obviously illustrated picture deceives nobody. A realistic "photograph" of an event that never happened, or of people who do not exist presented as your customers or staff, can deceive. If a reasonable viewer might take it for real, say that it is AI-generated. In the European Union, rules that took effect in August 2026 require disclosure of AI-generated or manipulated images that resemble real people, places or events. Many tools also embed a hidden marker in their images that identifies them as AI-made.

A simple test covers most cases: *would anyone feel misled if they learned how this image was made?* If yes, label it or do not use it.

## Worked example

Ines is a marketing manager at a company that delivers chilled food to restaurants. She is sending an invitation to a customer webinar on keeping food at safe temperatures during summer, and needs a header image by this afternoon. The stock library has nothing suitable.

**First attempt.** She types: "an image for a food safety webinar". She gets a glossy, realistic photograph of a smiling chef holding a clipboard in a spotless kitchen, with the words "FOOD SAFTEY WEBINAR" across the top.

There are three problems. The headline is misspelled. The chef looks like a real person and is nobody. The picture could be an advertisement for any company.

**A proper description.** She thinks about what she wants: something calm and clearly illustrated, with space for her own headline.

She uses the description from earlier in this lesson, with the delivery van at a restaurant's loading bay at dawn, flat illustration, open space on the right, no text, no logos, no people.

The result is close. The van is on the left, the light is warm, and the right side is open sky.

**Improving.** She makes three edits, one at a time:
- "Keep everything the same, but make the van plain white with no markings." The first version had a made-up company name on the side.
- "Add a small thermometer icon on the van's side showing a low temperature."
- "Same image, slightly less detail in the building."

After the second edit, the thermometer shows "3°C", and the numeral is cleanly drawn. She checks it carefully. Chilled food should be held at 5°C or below, so 3°C is a sensible figure to show.

**Text.** She adds the headline herself in her email tool, in the company font, over the open sky.

**Rights and labelling.** She runs through the list.
- *Ownership:* it is a header for one email. She does not need to own it.
- *Resemblance:* a generic van in a generic style. She asked for no logos and removed the invented company name.
- *Terms:* her company's paid plan allows commercial use.
- *Real people:* none.
- *Could it be mistaken for real?* No. It is plainly an illustration.

Her company's policy asks for AI-generated images in external material to be noted. She adds a small line at the foot of the email: "Header illustration created with AI."

She considers also generating a realistic photograph of "one of our drivers", and decides against it. A picture of an invented driver, presented as theirs, is exactly what the test rules out.

## In your tool

- **ChatGPT and Gemini** generate and edit images inside a chat.
- **Claude** does not generate photographs or paintings. It can create diagrams, icons and simple graphics, and it can read and discuss images you upload.
- **Design tools** such as Adobe's and Canva's have image generation built in, often with terms written for commercial use.

How many images you can make depends on your plan.

## Try it on your own work

1. Think of an image you need this month for a slide, a document or an invitation.
2. Write a description covering subject, setting and mood, style, composition and format, and what to leave out.
3. Generate it. Improve it with three single-change edits.
4. Check any text, the details and the accuracy of what is shown.
5. Run the five rights points. Decide whether it needs a label.
6. Find out what your organisation's policy says about AI-generated images. If there is none, ask.

## Ignore for now

- Specialist image tools with dozens of settings. The assistant you already use is enough to begin.
- Elaborate prompt formulas and long lists of style keywords.
- Generating images for things you need to own or protect, such as logos. Use a designer.
