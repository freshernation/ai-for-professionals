# Video

## Outcome

After this lesson you can choose the right kind of AI help for a work video, plan and produce a short one, and stay honest about what in it is real.

## Why it matters

Video explains some things better than any document can: how a product works, what a new process looks like, a message from a person people want to see. AI has lowered the cost of nearly every step of making one. Video is also the form in which AI can deceive most convincingly. A fabricated clip of a real person saying something they never said is now easy to make. You need a clear view of what the tools do well, what they do badly, and where the lines are.

## The idea

### Four kinds of AI help with video

They are very different. Most confusion comes from mixing them up.

**1. Generated clips.**
You describe a scene, or supply an image, and the tool creates a few seconds of moving video, sometimes with sound. The results can be striking. The limits are real:
- clips are short, a matter of seconds, and longer pieces are built by joining them;
- people, objects and settings drift between clips, so consistency is hard;
- text, hands and physical detail go wrong;
- a usable clip often takes several attempts.

At work, generated clips suit short illustrative footage: an abstract background, a scene that sets a mood, a visual metaphor. They do not suit showing your actual product, premises or people.

**2. Presenter or avatar videos.**
You supply a script and choose a digital presenter, who delivers it to camera. It is widely used for training and internal updates. The presenter may be a stock character supplied by the tool, or a digital copy of a real person made with their recorded consent.

**3. AI-assisted editing.**
You record real footage and AI does the tedious work. It removes pauses and filler words, adds captions, cuts a long recording into short clips, cleans up sound, reframes for different screens, and translates. This is the most useful kind for most professionals, and the least risky, because what the viewer sees is real.

**4. Screen recordings with AI support.**
You record your screen while showing how something works. AI writes the script, generates narration or cleans up yours, adds captions, and trims. This is ideal for software walk-throughs and process explanations.

The more that depends on the viewer's trust, the more of the video should be real. Use types 3 and 4 by default, and reach for types 1 and 2 with care.

### How to make a short video

**Start with the script.** Nearly every weak video has a weak script.

- **One message, one audience, one action.** What should the viewer know or do afterwards?
- **Keep it short.** People speak at roughly 130 to 150 words a minute, so a ninety-second video is about 200 words. Ask your assistant to cut to length.
- **Write for the ear,** and make the point in the first ten seconds.

```
Write a script for a 90-second video for [audience] explaining [topic].
The one thing they should do afterwards is [action]. About 200 words,
spoken English, short sentences. Open with the benefit to them.
Then give me a shot list: for each part of the script, what is on
screen.
```

**Plan the pictures.** The shot list says what the viewer sees during each part of the script. Decide which shots are real and which, if any, are generated.

**Record.** A phone, a window for light and a quiet room are enough.

**Edit with AI.** Remove pauses, add captions, trim to length. Always add captions. Many people watch without sound, and some cannot hear it.

**Review all of it.** Watch the whole video with the sound on, and once more with it off. Check every caption, every name and every figure on screen.

### Rights and disclosure, plainly

None of this is legal advice. Follow your organisation's policy.

**1. A person's likeness needs their consent.**
Do not create video of a real, identifiable person without their clear agreement. That covers colleagues, leaders, customers and public figures. For a digital copy of someone, including yourself, agreement should cover what it will say, where it will appear, for how long, and who approves each script. Reputable tools require the person to record a consent statement. The tool's check covers the tool's own position. You still need the agreement yourself.

**2. Never fabricate people or events that viewers will take as real.**
An invented "customer" praising your product is a false testimonial, whoever or whatever produced it. So is a generated scene presented as footage of your factory, or a clip of a real person saying words they never said.

**3. Label synthetic video.**
If a presenter is digital, or footage is generated, say so on screen or in the description. In the European Union, rules that took effect in August 2026 require disclosure of AI-generated or manipulated video that resembles real people, places or events. Several tools also embed a hidden marker or attach provenance information to what they generate.

The test is the same as for images: *would anyone feel misled if they learned how this was made?*

## Worked example

Diego is an account executive at a company that sells booking software to physiotherapy clinics. A new feature lets patients reschedule their own appointments by text message. Customers keep asking how it works, and Diego wants a ninety-second video he can send to all forty of his accounts.

**The script.** He gives his assistant the feature notes and writes:

```
Write a script for a 90-second video for physiotherapy clinic managers
who already use our software. It explains the new patient
self-rescheduling feature. The one thing they should do afterwards is
switch it on in Settings. About 200 words, spoken English. Open with
the benefit to them. Then give me a shot list.
```

The script opens: "How many calls does your front desk take each week from patients who just want to move an appointment?" It runs to 210 words. The shot list proposes: Diego to camera for the opening; a screen recording of the patient's text message; a screen recording of the clinic's calendar updating; the settings page; and Diego to camera for the close.

He checks every claim against the feature notes. One line says patients can reschedule "up to an hour before". The notes say the clinic sets the cut-off, and the default is four hours. He corrects it.

**Deciding what is real.** A colleague in marketing suggests two additions: a generated clip of "a happy clinic manager" saying how much time the feature has saved her, and a digital presenter in place of Diego, so that the video can be made in Spanish too.

Diego thinks about each one.

- *The generated clinic manager.* She would be an invented person giving a testimonial. His customers are clinic managers, and they would take her for one of their own. That is a fabricated endorsement. He says no. He asks a real customer from the pilot whether she would record twenty seconds on her phone. She agrees, and signs the company's release form.
- *The digital presenter.* His customers know him, and that is why they open his emails. A stranger's synthetic face would lose that. He records himself. For the Spanish version he will use the same footage with translated captions, checked by a Spanish-speaking colleague.

**Recording.** He records the two pieces to camera on his phone by a window, in three takes each. He records the screen walk-through with a test account, so that no real patient names appear.

**Editing.** He loads everything into an AI-assisted editor. It removes his pauses and two false starts, generates captions, and trims the result to ninety-four seconds. He asks his assistant which four seconds to cut, and removes a repeated sentence.

He uses one generated element: a five-second animated title card with the feature's name, in the company colours. It represents nothing real and deceives nobody.

**Review.** He watches with the sound on, then off. One caption reads "physio therapy" as two words, and another has "4 ours". He fixes both.

**Labelling.** Nothing in the video pretends to be something it is not. Diego and the customer are real, and the screen recording is the real product. Company policy asks for AI assistance to be noted, so the description says: "Edited and captioned with AI tools."

He sends it that afternoon.

## In your tool

- **ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini** all help with scripts, shot lists and reviewing a transcript. Gemini can generate short video clips on eligible plans.
- **Generated clips, presenter videos and AI-assisted editing** are mostly separate products, and this market changes quickly.

Check what your organisation has approved before you upload footage of people or of your screen.

## Try it on your own work

1. Pick something you explain repeatedly that would be clearer shown.
2. Write the one message, the audience and the one action.
3. Ask for a 90-second script and a shot list. Check every claim in it.
4. Decide, shot by shot, what will be real. Aim for all of it.
5. Record on your phone or record your screen. Use an AI-assisted editor, or your recording tool's built-in features, to trim and caption.
6. Watch with sound on and off. Correct the captions. Decide what label it needs.

## Ignore for now

- Generating whole videos from a text description.
- Making a digital copy of yourself.
- Cinematic quality.
