# Audio and voice

## Outcome

After this lesson you can use your voice to work with an assistant, turn written material into something you can listen to, and produce narration for work, while staying on the right side of the rules about other people's voices.

## Why it matters

Most people think of AI as something you type into. Voice changes when and how you can use it. You can talk through a problem on a walk, listen to a long report on a train, or rehearse a hard conversation aloud. For anyone who makes training material or presentations, a clear narration now takes minutes and needs no studio. The same technology can copy a real person's voice from a short recording. That makes consent a serious matter, and it has made voice a tool for fraud. You need to know the uses and the limits.

## The idea

There are three ways to use audio. You can speak to the assistant, listen to what it produces, or use it to produce audio for other people.

### 1. Speak: talking to your assistant

There are two forms of this, and they suit different jobs.

**Dictation.** You talk and the assistant turns it into text. This is the fastest way to get your thoughts down, because people say far more than they type. It suits rough notes for a document, a recap after a meeting, and a full briefing for a task.

**Voice conversation.** You and the assistant talk back and forth, and you can interrupt it. This suits:

- **Thinking aloud.** "I'm going to talk through a decision. Ask me questions when something is unclear."
- **Rehearsal.** "Play my customer. I'll practise explaining the price increase. Push back as they would."
- **Being tutored.** Answering questions aloud shows what you actually know.
- **Hands-free moments.** Driving, walking, cooking.

Two cautions. Check anything factual, since voice conversations may use a less capable model. And think about who can hear you.

### 2. Listen: turning text into audio

- **Read aloud.** Most assistants and many devices can read any text to you. This is useful for reviewing your own writing, since you hear mistakes your eyes pass over, and for getting through long documents.
- **Audio overviews.** Some tools can turn a set of documents into a spoken discussion between two voices, like a short podcast about your material. It is a good way to get oriented in a subject. It is a summary, with a summary's faults. It simplifies, it can overstate, and it is hard to check line by line. Treat it as an introduction and go to the documents for anything you will rely on.

### 3. Produce: making audio for others

**Narration from a script.** Text-to-speech has become very good. You supply a script, choose a voice, and get a natural-sounding recording. It works well for training modules, product walk-throughs, video voice-overs and accessibility versions of written material. To get a good result:

- **Write for the ear.** Use short sentences with one idea each. Read the script aloud yourself first.
- **Spell out what might be mispronounced.** Names, product names, abbreviations and numbers need care. Many tools let you set how a word is said.
- **Listen to the whole thing.** Odd stresses, a mispronounced name, or a pause in the wrong place are common and easy to fix.

**Translation.** Tools can produce your narration in other languages. Have a fluent speaker check anything important.

**Music.** Tools can generate background music from a description. Check the licence (see below).

### Rights and disclosure, plainly

None of this is legal advice, and your organisation's policy comes first.

**1. A voice belongs to a person.**
A person's voice identifies them, as their face does. Tools can now copy a voice from a short sample.

- **Only clone your own voice, or one whose owner has given clear, written agreement** for that specific use.
- **Never imitate a real person**, whether a colleague, a chief executive, a celebrity or a customer, without that agreement. It makes no difference if the purpose is harmless or internal.
- **Agreement should cover the detail:** what the voice will say, where it will be used, for how long, and who approves the scripts. A person who agrees to narrate one training module has not agreed to anything else.
- Reputable tools require consent and verification for voice cloning. The responsibility is still yours.

Most work needs no cloning at all. A stock voice supplied by the tool is the simple and safe choice.

**2. Say when a voice is synthetic.**
If listeners might assume a real person recorded it, tell them: "Narration generated with AI." This matters most where trust is involved, such as messages from leaders, customer communications, and anything presented as a personal message.

**3. Check the licence for music.**
Free plans on music tools often allow personal use only. Paid plans usually grant commercial rights, on terms that differ and have been changing. The legal position of AI-generated music is still being worked out. For anything public, read the current terms, or use music from a library with a clear licence.

**4. Voice is now a tool for fraud.**
Criminals use cloned voices to impersonate managers and family members, usually to request an urgent payment or a password. A familiar voice on a call no longer proves who is speaking. If a spoken request is unusual, urgent and involves money or access, check it through a second channel that you already trust, such as a number you have on file. Tell your team to do the same.

## Worked example

Nomvula is a learning and development specialist in the HR team of a hotel group. She has written a six-minute onboarding module on guest safety for new staff, and needs it narrated in English, Spanish and Portuguese by the end of the week.

**The script.** She reads her script aloud and stumbles on three sentences. They are too long for the ear, so she splits them. She asks her assistant: "Rewrite this for narration. Short sentences, spoken English, nothing lost. Mark any word that might be mispronounced." It flags the names of two hotels, the abbreviation "AED", and "0800" in a telephone number.

**The voice.** Her director has an idea. The chief executive has a warm and well-known voice, and there are hours of him on video. Could she clone it, so that he "welcomes" every new starter?

Nomvula says no, and explains her reasons. He has not agreed. It would put words in his mouth in three languages, two of which he does not speak. New staff who later found out that the welcome was synthetic would trust the rest of the programme less. She suggests something else. The chief executive records a real thirty-second welcome on his phone, in his own words, and a stock voice narrates the module itself. He does it the next morning.

For the module she picks a calm, clear stock voice from her company's approved text-to-speech tool.

**Listening.** She generates the English narration and listens to all six minutes. She finds four things. One hotel's name is said wrongly. "AED" is read as a word and not as three letters. There is an odd pause in the middle of one sentence. And "0800" comes out as "eight hundred". She sets the pronunciations, rewrites the telephone number as words, and regenerates.

**Translation.** The tool produces Spanish and Portuguese versions. She sends each script and audio file to a colleague who speaks the language natively. The Spanish reviewer changes two phrases that are correct and too formal for hotel staff. The Portuguese reviewer finds that an instruction about fire doors has become ambiguous in translation. That is a safety instruction, and it matters. Nomvula corrects both and regenerates.

**Labelling.** On the module's first screen she adds: "Narration generated with AI and reviewed by the Learning team. Welcome message recorded by [the chief executive]."

All three languages are finished in two days. The time went on the script, on listening to every minute, and on the two reviews, one of which caught a safety instruction that had lost its meaning.

## In your tool

- **ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini** all accept dictation and offer spoken conversation in their apps. Each can read its replies aloud.
- **Gemini's NotebookLM** produces spoken overviews from a set of sources.
- **Narration and voice tools** are mostly separate products. Many video, presentation and e-learning tools include text-to-speech.

Features and limits depend on your plan and your device.

## Try it on your own work

1. Dictate, instead of typing, the briefing for your next task. Compare the length with what you would have typed.
2. Have a five-minute spoken conversation: think aloud about a decision, or rehearse a conversation you have coming up.
3. Have something you wrote read aloud to you. Note what you would change.
4. If you have a set of documents to get through, try an audio overview. Then check one of its claims against the documents.
5. Take a short script, rewrite it for the ear, and generate a narration with a stock voice. Listen to every second.
6. Agree with your team how you will verify an unusual spoken request for money or access.

## Ignore for now

- Cloning your own voice. Stock voices cover most needs.
- Generating songs.
- Real-time translation of live calls.

## In the module session

The instructor runs a spoken rehearsal live, produces a narration and corrects its errors with the group, plays an audio overview and checks it against its sources, and works through consent cases, including the request to clone a leader's voice.
