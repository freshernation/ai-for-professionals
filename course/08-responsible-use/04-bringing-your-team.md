# Bringing your team along

## Outcome

After this lesson you can help a team start using AI well: agree a few rules, show real examples, deal honestly with people's concerns, and stop poor AI output being passed around.

## Why it matters

One person who uses AI well gets a few hours back. A team that uses it well changes how the work gets done. A team that uses it badly produces a flood of long, plausible documents that nobody checked. What separates the two is whether people have agreed how they will work, whether it is safe to talk about what goes wrong, and whether anyone has shown the others how. You can help with this whether or not you are the manager.

## The idea

### Start with a real problem

"We should all be using AI" produces a shrug. "The weekly client report takes each of us two hours, and I think we can halve that" produces interest. Choose one task the team shares and dislikes.

### Agree the rules before the methods

People hold back when they do not know what is allowed. Half an hour spent agreeing a short set of team rules removes most of that hesitation. Five questions cover it.

1. **Which tools?** Only those the organisation has approved.
2. **What information may go in?** Give examples from your own work, such as "client names: no; anonymised figures: yes".
3. **What must be checked, and by whom?** Whoever sends a piece of work has checked it, every time.
4. **When do we say AI was used?** Agree something simple, in line with company policy.
5. **What do we not use it for?** In most teams this includes decisions about people and anything sent to a customer unread.

Write the answers on one page. Review it after a month.

### Show, don't tell

A demonstration on a real task persuades people more than any presentation. Take a piece of the team's actual work and do it in front of them: the brief, the first poor result, the correction and the check. Then have each person try it on something of their own.

### Make it a habit to share

- **A shared prompt library.** A document where people put prompts that worked, with one line on what each is for.
- **Ten minutes in a regular meeting.** Ask two questions: what worked this week, and what went wrong?

The second question matters more than the first. A team that talks only about its successes learns slowly, and its members hide their mistakes.

### Take concerns seriously

People have real reasons for reluctance. Answer them honestly.

**"Will this replace me?"** Do not promise what you cannot know. You can say truthfully that the work of briefing, checking and judging is growing, and that people who do it well are becoming more valuable.

**"It makes mistakes."** It does. That is why the team has a checking rule, and it is a reason to use it with care. It is not a reason to refuse.

**"It feels like cheating."** If the thinking is yours and you stand behind the result, the tool matters no more than a spreadsheet does.

**"I've tried it and it was useless."** Ask what they tried. Often it was a bare request months ago. Sometimes they are right.

Aim for a team that uses it where it helps. Do not aim for the same level of use from everyone.

### Stop the slop

The biggest risk to a team is an unspoken shift of effort. One person generates a long, polished document in two minutes and sends it on without really reading it. The recipient then spends twenty minutes working out what it says and whether any of it is right. The sender saved time, and the team lost more than the sender saved.

A 2025 survey reported in *Harvard Business Review* gave this a name, "workslop", meaning AI-generated work that looks finished and does not actually move the task forward. Four in ten of the office workers surveyed said they had received some in the previous month, by their own report.

Team norms that prevent it:

- **The sender does the work.** You have read it, checked it and cut it down before anyone else sees it.
- **Shorter is a courtesy.** If AI made it longer, edit it back.
- **Do not forward raw output.** If you would not have written it, do not send it.
- **It is acceptable to send it back.** "I don't think this has been checked" should be an ordinary thing to say.

### Protect people who are still learning

A junior colleague who lets an assistant do the analysis does not learn how to analyse. In the short term the output looks fine. In two years the team has someone who cannot judge whether the assistant is right. For people still learning the job, agree which tasks they do themselves first, and encourage them to use AI to be questioned and tested on their understanding. It should not do the thinking for them.

### If you are not the manager

You can still lead by example. Do your own work well and openly, share a prompt that worked, and ask in a team meeting whether you could agree a few rules.

## Worked example

Farid leads a team of eight transport planners at a logistics company. The company rolled out an approved assistant three months ago, with an hour of online training. Two of his planners use it constantly. Four tried it once. Two have not opened it. Last week a customer complained about a route summary that contained a delivery window nobody had promised. It turned out to be an AI draft that had been sent unread.

He decides to treat this as a team matter, not as one person's failing.

**A real problem.** Every Friday each planner writes a performance summary for each of their customers, covering deliveries made, delays and reasons. It takes each of them about ninety minutes, and nobody likes it. He picks that.

**Rules first.** He gives thirty minutes of the Monday meeting to the five questions. The discussion is livelier than he expected.

- *Tools:* the company assistant only. One planner admits to using a personal account "because it's better". Farid says plainly that this has to stop, because customer data in a personal account breaks the company's agreements with its customers.
- *Information:* customer names and delivery data may go into the approved tool. Drivers' personal details may not.
- *Checking:* whoever sends it has checked every figure against the transport system. This is the rule that would have prevented last week's complaint.
- *Saying so:* within the team, people say when something is an unchecked AI draft.
- *Not for:* anything about a driver's performance or conduct.

**Showing.** On Wednesday he does one Friday summary live, on the big screen, for a real customer. His first prompt gives a generic paragraph. He adds the week's figures and two examples of good summaries, and the second version is good. Then he checks it, line by line, against the system. One delay is attributed to weather. The system says it was a vehicle fault. He corrects it in front of everyone.

"That's the part that matters," he says. "It took me twelve minutes, and three of those were checking." Each planner then does one of their own.

**Concerns.** Helga, who has twenty years' experience, is openly sceptical. "I can write these in my sleep, and mine are right." Farid asks her to time one each way. Hers takes seventy minutes by hand and twenty-five with the assistant, and she finds two errors in the draft. "It's faster," she says. "But I wouldn't trust anyone to send these without checking." Farid asks her to review the team's checking for the first month. She says yes.

Joon, the newest planner, is one of the constant users. His summaries are fluent, but when Farid asks him why a particular route ran late, he cannot say. He has been passing on what the assistant wrote. Farid asks him to write the delay analysis himself for the next month and use the assistant only for the wording, and pairs him with Helga for an hour a week.

**Sharing.** He starts a prompt library and adds ten minutes to the Monday meeting. In the first week nobody admits to anything going wrong. In the second, one of the confident users describes a summary that gave the wrong month's figures. After that, people talk more freely.

**Six weeks on.** The Friday summaries take about thirty-five minutes each. Six of the eight planners use the assistant for them. One prefers to write his own, and Farid leaves him to it. There have been no further customer complaints. Twice, a planner has sent a colleague's note back with "has this been checked?", and nobody took offence.

## Try it on your own work

1. Name one task that your team shares and dislikes.
2. Draft answers to the five questions for your team, on one page. Take it to the team for discussion.
3. Do one real task in front of at least one colleague, including the checking.
4. Start a shared document for prompts that worked. Put your best one in it.
5. Ask in a team meeting: "What has gone wrong with AI for anyone recently?" Answer first yourself.
6. Think of the most sceptical person on your team. What would they need to see?

## Ignore for now

- Mandating use, or measuring who uses it most.
- Large training programmes. One real task done well does more.


## In the module session

The instructor facilitates the drafting of a one-page team agreement, role-plays the difficult conversations with sceptical and over-reliant colleagues, and discusses what learners have seen work and fail in their own teams.
