# Measuring the time you actually save

## Outcome

After this lesson you can find out, with a simple two-week log, where AI really saves you time, where it costs you time, and what to change as a result.

## Why it matters

Ask people how much time AI saves them and most will give a confident figure. Very few have measured it. The feeling of speed is strong, because text appears at once and the work feels easier. The time spent briefing, checking and fixing is harder to notice. You need the true answer to know which uses to keep and which to drop.

## The idea

### Why feelings mislead

In a controlled study published in 2025, sixteen experienced software developers each completed tasks on projects they knew well, with AI tools allowed on some tasks and not on others, chosen at random. Afterwards they estimated that AI had made them about 20% faster. The measurements showed that they had taken 19% longer when AI was allowed.

This is one study of one kind of work, and other studies of other tasks have found real gains. The useful finding is the gap between what capable people felt and what the clock recorded. You may be wrong about your own speed too, and you cannot tell which way without measuring.

### What to count

**Time with AI** is everything the task took:
- writing the brief and gathering the material;
- waiting;
- reading and steering;
- **checking**: tracing facts, recomputing numbers;
- **fixing**: correcting errors and rewriting what was not right.

**Time without AI** is how long the task would have taken done your usual way. Use your honest experience of doing it before. Better still, do one or two of each kind without AI during the fortnight and time them.

**Net saving** is time without, minus time with. It can be negative.

**Things that do not appear on the clock:**
- *Rework later.* An error that surfaces next week, or a colleague who has to come back to you.
- *Other people's time.* A longer document that takes your reader more time. A draft that someone else has to correct.
- *Quality.* Was the result better, the same or worse?
- *Things you would not otherwise have done.* An analysis you would have skipped, or a second draft you would not have written. These are real benefits. They are not time saved.

### The two-week log

Keep it simple, or you will not keep it at all. Use one line per task, written at the time:

| Date | Task type | Time with AI (all in) | Time without (estimate) | Quality | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- Write the line when you finish the task. Do not leave it to the end of the day.
- Include the tasks where AI did not help. Leaving those out is the most common way to fool yourself.
- Group tasks into a handful of types. You are looking for patterns by type.

### Reading the result

Add up the figures by task type and sort the types into three groups.

**Clear wins.** These show a large saving, with quality the same or better. *Keep them. Save the prompt. Use it every time.*

**Marginal.** These show a small saving, or a saving that depends on the day. *Either improve the method or stop bothering.* Often the checking is taking most of the time, and a better setup would cut it, for example by loading the reference document once in a project.

**Losses.** These took longer or came out worse. *Stop, or change the approach.* Typical causes are that the task needs judgement the assistant lacks, that checking takes as long as doing it, or that you end up rewriting everything.

There are nearly always some of each. The overall figure matters less than knowing which task falls in which group.

### Being honest about it

- **Report a range.** "Roughly two to three hours a week, mostly on drafting" is something you can defend. "A 40% productivity gain" is not.
- **Say what you counted.** Did you include checking time?
- **Do not extrapolate.** Two hours saved on your tasks does not mean two hours for a colleague with different work.
- **Measure again in a few months.** The tools change, and so does your skill with them. A task that was a loss in the spring may be a win by the autumn.

### What happens to saved time

Saved time gets absorbed into the week unless you decide in advance what it is for: the work you never get to, or more care on the work that matters most.

## Worked example

Aroha is an HR advisor in a company of four hundred people. She has used her company's approved assistant for six months. When her manager asks whether the licences are worth renewing, she says, "Definitely. It saves me about a day a week." Her manager asks how she knows. She does not know.

She keeps a log for two weeks. Each time she finishes a task where she used the assistant, or tried to, she writes one line. For the "without" column she uses her experience from before she had the tool, and she does one advert and one set of notes the old way to check her memory.

**The log, totalled by task type:**

| Task type | Number | Minutes each, with AI | Minutes each, without | Saving each | Total saving |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Job adverts | 6 | 15 | 45 | 30 | 180 |
| Interview question sets | 4 | 15 | 40 | 25 | 100 |
| Meeting notes from her rough notes | 8 | 8 | 20 | 12 | 96 |
| Replies to managers' policy questions | 14 | 10 | 12 | 2 | 28 |
| Absence data analysis | 2 | 120 | 90 | −30 | −60 |
| Disciplinary outcome letters | 3 | 70 | 60 | −10 | −30 |
| **Total** | **37** | | | | **314** |

That is 314 minutes over two weeks, a little over five hours, or about two and a half hours a week. She had said a day.

One item is not in the table. In the second week a manager came back to her because a policy answer she had sent, which was AI-drafted and lightly checked, gave the wrong notice period for a change to working hours. Putting that right, including a call to the employee concerned, took forty minutes. With that included the total falls to 274 minutes, or a little over two hours a week.

**Reading it.**

*Clear wins: adverts, interview questions and meeting notes.* Together these save 376 minutes. They are structured, low in risk and easy for her to judge. She saves her three prompts and makes them her standard method.

*Marginal: policy replies.* These save two minutes each. Most of the time goes on checking the draft against the policy handbook. That check is necessary, as the notice-period error showed, since it came from skimping the check on one of these replies. She has been pasting sections of the handbook into each chat. She sets up a project with the whole handbook loaded and an instruction to quote the relevant section with every answer, so that she can check in seconds. She will measure again in a month.

*Losses: data analysis and letters.*
- The absence analysis took longer with AI both times. The export from the HR system is untidy, the assistant's first results were wrong, and finding out why took longer than her usual spreadsheet method. She goes back to her spreadsheet for now.
- The disciplinary letters took longer because she rewrote nearly every sentence. They are sensitive and carry legal weight, and one draft implied a finding that had not been made. She stops using it for these altogether.

**What she tells her manager.**

"I measured it for two weeks. It saves me between two and three hours a week, counting the time I spend checking, nearly all on adverts, interview questions and meeting notes. It costs me time on data analysis and disciplinary letters, and I've stopped using it for those. The licence is worth renewing for me. I wouldn't assume the same figure for the rest of the team without their logging it too."

Aroha decides what the two and a half hours are for. There is a backlog of exit interviews that nobody has analysed for a year. She blocks Thursday afternoons for it.

## Try it on your own work

1. Write down your guess. How many hours a week does AI save you?
2. Set up the log: date, task type, time with AI, time without, quality and notes.
3. Keep it for two weeks. Write each line as you finish the task. Include the tasks where it did not help.
4. Do one or two tasks without AI, and time them, to check your estimates.
5. Total by task type. Sort the types into clear wins, marginal and losses.
6. Compare the total with your guess. Then decide what to keep, what to improve, what to stop, and what the saved time is for.

## Ignore for now

- Precise productivity percentages.
- Measuring the whole organisation. Start with yourself.
- Published claims about how much time AI saves "the average worker". Your tasks are not average.

## In the module session

The instructor reviews the logs that learners bring, puts their guesses beside their measurements, and discusses what the research does and does not show about AI and productivity.

1. What must "time with AI" include?
2. Why should the log include tasks where AI did not help?
3. In the example, why did the policy replies save so little, and what did Aroha change?
