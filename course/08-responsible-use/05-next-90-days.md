# Your next 90 days

## Outcome

After this lesson you have a written plan for the next three months, with three tasks, three milestones and a way of keeping up as the tools change.

## Why it matters

Reading about AI changes nothing, and a course about it changes little more if you do not act on it. The change comes from using it on your own work every week until it is a habit. Most people finish a course with good intentions and are back to their old ways within a month. Ninety days is long enough to make a few uses automatic and short enough to plan properly.

## The idea

### Choose three tasks

Three is enough to matter and few enough to keep up. Pick tasks that are:

- **frequent**, weekly if possible and monthly at least, so that the habit forms and the saving adds up;
- **suited to it**, meaning drafting, summarising, structuring, preparing or analysing something you can check;
- **yours to change**, so that you need nobody's permission.

Leave out, for now, tasks that are high in stakes and hard to check.

### Set three milestones

A milestone is something you have produced or done. A statement of intent does not count. "Get better at prompting" cannot be checked. "A tested, saved prompt for the weekly report, used four weeks running" can.

**Day 30: three things working.**
For each of your three tasks, a saved prompt or method that you have used on real work, three times or more where the task is weekly, with a checking routine that fits the stakes.

**Day 60: one step further, and a measurement.**
Take on one thing that is harder than what you do now, such as a multi-step task handed to an agent or a small tool you have built. Keep a two-week log of time with and without.

**Day 90: shared and reviewed.**
Show at least one colleague what you have learned, on a real task. Review your three tasks and decide what to keep, change or drop. Write the plan for the next ninety days.

### Put it where your work lives

A plan left in a notes file will be forgotten.

- **Calendar.** Put the three milestone dates in as appointments with yourself, and add thirty minutes each week to keep the plan moving.
- **Tell someone.** Tell your manager or a colleague what you are doing and when. A commitment made aloud is more likely to be kept.

### Expect to fall behind

A busy fortnight will come and the plan will slip. That is normal. When it happens, do not abandon the plan. Adjust it. Move a milestone, drop a task, or halve the ambition. A plan that has been revised and finished is worth much more than a perfect one given up in week five.

### Keeping up without chasing everything

The tools will change during your ninety days. Do not try to follow all of it.

**One source, not ten.** Choose one reliable place for updates, such as your provider's release notes, one newsletter, or a knowledgeable colleague. Ignore the rest.

**A monthly half-hour.** Once a month, try one thing you have not tried before: a new feature, or a task you assumed was beyond it. That is how you find out what has changed, and reading about it does not do the same job.

**Re-test old conclusions.** "It can't do that" has a short life. Something that failed six months ago may work now. Every so often, try again the things you gave up on.

**Hold on to what does not change.** Features come and go. Certain things stay true:

- an assistant knows only what is in front of it, so give it context;
- it writes what is plausible, which is not always what is true, so check in proportion to the stakes;
- say what you want, for whom, and within what limits;
- a first answer is a draft;
- anything it does in your name is yours;
- sending, paying, deleting and publishing need a person;
- other people's information comes with other people's rules.

Someone who understands these can pick up any new tool in an afternoon.

### Where to go deeper

After ninety days you will know what you want more of: depth in your own field, data analysis, agents and automation, or helping others adopt it. Choose one.

### What the goal is

The aim was never to use AI as much as possible. It is to spend less time on what a machine can draft, and more on judgement, on relationships and on the things only you know.

## Worked example

Sunita is a finance manager at a housing association. She leads a team of four. She has been using her organisation's approved assistant on and off for a few months, mostly for emails. She wants it to make a real difference before the year-end.

**Building the plan.** She asks the assistant to help, and to interview her first:

```
I want a 90-day plan for making AI a real part of my work as a
finance manager. Before you suggest anything, interview me, one
question at a time: what my week consists of, what takes the most
time, what I've tried, and what I must be careful about. Then propose
three tasks to focus on and a milestone for day 30, 60 and 90. Each
milestone must be something I produce or do.
```

After eight questions, the plan takes shape. The assistant had suggested drafting responses to audit queries. She judges that too high in stakes to begin with and swaps it.

**Three tasks.**
1. The monthly variance commentary: explaining to budget holders why spending differs from budget. It takes four hours a month.
2. The board finance summary: turning a twenty-page pack into a one-page brief. It takes two hours a month.
3. Supplier and budget-holder queries: about fifteen routine emails a week.

**Milestones.**

*Day 30:* a saved, tested prompt for each of the three, each used on real work, with her checking rule written down: every figure traced to the ledger before anything leaves her desk.

*Day 60:* one step further. She will hand the preparation for the monthly supplier reconciliation to an agent, with a pilot first and nothing sent, and keep a two-week time log.

*Day 90:* a thirty-minute session for her team, in which she does the variance commentary live, including the check, and a one-page draft of team rules.

**Where it lives.** She puts the three dates in her calendar and blocks thirty minutes every Friday at 4pm. She tells the finance director what she is doing and asks to show him the result at day 90.

**What happened.**

*Weeks 1 to 4.* The email prompt works from the first day. The variance commentary takes three attempts: the first drafts explain every variance at the same length, and she adds a rule to cover only the significant ones. Day 30 is met a week late.

*Weeks 5 to 7.* Month-end is followed by an unplanned audit visit. She does nothing on the plan for a fortnight, and in week seven she nearly gives it up.

She adjusts it. She moves the reconciliation agent to the next ninety days and replaces it with something smaller: loading the finance procedures manual into a project, so that routine questions can be answered with a quotation from the manual. She keeps the time log.

*Weeks 8 to 10.* The log shows about three hours a week saved, nearly all on the commentary and the emails. The board summary saves little, because the finance director rewrites it whatever she does. They agree that she will send him structured points and he will write the summary himself.

*Weeks 11 to 13.* She runs the session for her team. She makes a mistake with a cost-centre code and catches it in the check, which turns out to be the most useful part. The team changes two of her draft rules.

**Day 90.** She reviews the plan against what she did:
- *Keep:* the commentary and email prompts, and the procedures project.
- *Change:* the board summary, which is now structured points only.
- *Carry forward:* the reconciliation agent.
- *Add:* a monthly half-hour to try one new thing. In week nine she found by accident that the assistant could now read the scanned invoices it had failed on in the spring.

The next plan takes her twenty minutes to write.

## Try it on your own work

1. Choose your three tasks. Check that each is frequent, suited to AI and yours to change.
2. Write a milestone for day 30, day 60 and day 90. Each must be something you produce or do.
3. Have your assistant interview you about the plan and challenge it. Change what does not fit.
4. Put the three dates and a weekly half-hour in your calendar.
5. Tell one person what you are doing and when.
6. Choose your one source for updates, and schedule your first monthly half-hour.

## Ignore for now

- Planning beyond ninety days.
- Every new tool and announcement.
- Comparing your progress with anyone else's.

## In the module session

The instructor reviews each learner's plan, with the group checking that every milestone is something produced or done, and closes the course by returning to the goal each learner set at the start.

## Check yourself

1. What three properties should each of your chosen tasks have?
2. What makes a milestone a good one?
3. When Sunita fell behind in week seven, what did she do, and why was that the right choice?
