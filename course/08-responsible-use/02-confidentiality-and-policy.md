# Confidentiality, copyright and company policy

## Outcome

After this lesson you can answer three questions before using AI on any piece of work: may this information go in, may I use what comes out, and what does my employer require?

## Why it matters

The most useful thing you can give an assistant is real material from your work. That material often belongs to someone else. It may be your employer's, a client's or a customer's, or it may be personal information about real people. What comes out raises questions too: who owns it, whether it copies something, and whether you are expected to say how it was made. Most people who get into difficulty with AI at work simply pasted something they should not have. None of this lesson is legal advice. Your organisation's policy and its legal team have the final word.

## The idea

### Question 1: May this go in?

**Sort the information.**

- **Public or harmless.** Published material, general questions, your own notes with nothing sensitive in them. This is fine in any reputable tool.
- **Internal.** Your organisation's documents, plans, figures and code. Use only tools your employer has approved, and only in the way the policy allows.
- **Restricted.** Personal information about identifiable people. Passwords and security details. Anything covered by a confidentiality agreement or by legal privilege. Unannounced results or deals. Regulated data such as health or payment records. This goes in only where your employer has approved that tool for that kind of data.

**The account matters as much as the tool.** The same assistant on a personal account and on your employer's business account works under different terms. Business accounts normally come with a contract that covers how data is used, kept and protected.

**Other people's information comes with other people's rules.**

- *Personal data.* Information about identifiable people is protected by law in most countries. Your organisation may use it only for proper purposes and only with suppliers it has approved. Pasting a customer list or an employee's record into a tool that has not been approved may be a breach, whatever the tool then does with it.
- *Client and partner information.* Contracts and confidentiality agreements often limit who may see or process the information. Some forbid passing it to any outside service without consent. Your employer's approval of a tool does not override a promise made to a client.
- *Trade secrets.* In 2023, engineers at a large electronics company pasted confidential source code into a public chatbot to get help with it, and the company restricted such tools in response. Nobody intended harm.

**When it cannot go in, change it.** Replace names with roles, round or scale the figures, remove identifiers, or describe the situation without pasting the document.

### Question 2: May I use what comes out?

**You may not own it.** In several countries, copyright protects work with a human author, and purely AI-generated material may have no protection. That matters for anything your organisation needs to own, such as a logo, a product design or published creative work.

**It may contain someone else's work.** An assistant can produce text, images or code that closely matches existing protected material. Reduce the risk as follows:
- do not ask it to reproduce or imitate a specific work, author or brand;
- do not paste in copyrighted material you have no right to use, such as a paid report, to be reworked into your own document. Many licences forbid uploading their content to AI tools at all;
- if an output looks familiar, do not use it;

**Say how it was made, where that is expected.** Your policy, a client contract or a regulator may require it. Where no rule applies, disclose AI's part whenever the reader would feel misled to learn of it later. In every case you take full responsibility for the content.

### Question 3: What does my employer require?

**Find the policy.** Many organisations now have one. It typically covers:

- which tools are approved, and for what;
- what information may and may not be entered;
- that a person must review AI output before it is used;
- when AI use must be disclosed;
- uses that are not allowed, such as decisions about people without human review;
- who to ask, and how to report a problem.

**If there is no policy,** ask in writing which tools are permitted and for what. Until you have an answer, keep internal and restricted information out.

**If you make a mistake,** report it quickly. Early reporting usually means the information can be deleted and the matter closed.

### A thirty-second routine

Before you paste or upload, ask:

1. Whose information is this?
2. Is this tool, on this account, approved for it?
3. Is there a promise to a client, a customer or an individual that this would break?
4. If I am unsure, can I strip it down, or should I ask?

## Worked example

Mateus is an account manager at an IT services firm. His largest client's contract is up for renewal, and he has a week to prepare the proposal. His firm provides an approved business assistant and has a two-page AI policy that he has never read closely. He reads it now.

**The policy.** It says that the approved assistant may be used for internal information. Client information may be entered only if the client's contract permits processing by third-party services. Personal data requires approval from the data protection lead. AI-generated content in client documents must be reviewed by the author. Licensed third-party content must not be uploaded.

**The client's data.** He wants to give the assistant the current contract, the client's usage figures for the year, and a set of support tickets, and ask for a renewal proposal.

He goes through the routine.
- *Whose information is it?* The client's, and in the tickets, the client's employees'.
- *Is the tool approved?* Yes, for internal information. For client information, it depends on the contract.
- *Is there a promise?* He opens the contract. Clause 11 says that the client's confidential information must not be disclosed to any third-party processor without the client's written consent. The assistant's provider is a third-party processor.

So the contract and the detailed usage figures cannot go in as they are. The support tickets also contain the names and email addresses of the client's staff, which is personal data.

Asking the client for consent would take longer than a week. He strips the material down.
- He writes his own summary of the contract's commercial terms, with no client name.
- He turns the usage figures into percentages and trends: "usage of service A grew by about a third over the year, and service B was flat".
- He does not upload the tickets. He reads them and writes five lines on the recurring themes, with no names.

He checks with his manager that a de-identified summary of this kind is acceptable under clause 11. He gives the assistant that summary and asks for a proposal structure and a first draft. He puts the real figures and the client's name in himself afterwards.

**The analyst report.** His firm subscribes to an industry analyst's reports, and a recent one has market figures that would strengthen the proposal. He is about to upload the PDF when he remembers the policy's line about licensed content. The subscription terms prohibit uploading reports to AI tools. He reads the report himself and uses two figures with attribution, as the licence allows.

**The output.** It is a proposal, not a product, so ownership is not a concern. One paragraph describing "our methodology" reads like standard industry boilerplate, and he rewrites it to describe what his firm actually does. The policy requires review by the author, so he reviews every page and checks each figure against his own records.

**The mistake.** While reading the policy, he remembers something. A month ago he was in a hurry and pasted a list of this client's contacts, with names, job titles and email addresses, into his personal assistant account at home to draft a set of emails.

That was personal data, in an unapproved tool, on a personal account. He emails the data protection lead that afternoon and says what happened and when. She asks him to delete the conversation, records the incident and thanks him for raising it. Nothing further comes of it. Had it surfaced six months later during a client audit, the conversation would have gone very differently.

The proposal goes out on time.

## Try it on your own work

1. Find your organisation's AI policy and read it. If there is none, send one email asking which tools are permitted and for what.
2. List the kinds of information you handle. Sort them into public, internal and restricted.
3. For one client, customer or partner, find out what your agreement with them says about sharing their information with outside services.
4. Think back over the last month. Did anything go into a tool, or an account, where it should not have? If so, report it.
5. Take one restricted item you would like help with, and write a stripped-down version.
6. Check the terms of one subscription or licensed source you use. May its content be uploaded to AI tools?

## Ignore for now

- The fine detail of copyright litigation. It is unsettled, and your policy is the working answer.
- Trying to interpret the law yourself where your organisation has people whose job that is.
