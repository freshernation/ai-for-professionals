# Your first useful conversation

## Outcome

After this lesson you can run a complete working conversation with an assistant: start it well, steer it, check what it gives you, and leave with something you actually use.

## Why it matters

Many people's first experience of AI is a single question and a single answer. It looks like a search engine, the answer is mediocre, and they close the tab. The value shows up when you treat it as a conversation with a capable assistant who knows nothing about your situation until you explain it. This lesson walks through one such conversation from start to finish.

## The idea

A useful conversation has four moves.

**1. Brief it as you would a new colleague.**
Imagine handing the task to a bright temp who started this morning. You would not say "sort out the meeting". You would say who you are, what you are trying to achieve, who it is for, and what a good result looks like. Give the assistant the same. Three or four sentences is enough to begin.

**2. Give it the material.**
If the task is about a document, an email, or a set of notes, paste it in or attach it, subject to what is safe to share. An assistant working from your actual material is far more accurate than one working from a description.

**3. Steer with follow-ups.**
The first answer is a draft, not a verdict. Talk back to it:

- "Shorter. Half this length."
- "Too formal. Write it the way I'd say it to a colleague."
- "Keep points 1 and 3, drop the rest, and add one about cost."
- "What have I not considered?"
- "Explain the second point again, more simply."

Within one chat the assistant keeps the whole conversation in view, so each follow-up builds on what came before. Three rounds of steering usually beat one long, careful prompt.

**4. Check, then use.**
Before you rely on the result, read it properly. Is every fact in it something you supplied or can verify? Did it quietly invent a detail? Does it say what you mean? Then edit it into your own words where it matters. You are responsible for whatever you send, sign or present.

**A few habits that help from day one**

- **One task per chat.** Start a new chat for a new topic. Old, unrelated material in the conversation gets in the way.
- **Say what you don't want.** "No bullet points", "no jargon", "don't invent figures".
- **Ask it to ask you.** "Before you start, ask me anything you need to know." This often surfaces something you forgot to say.
- **Keep what works.** When a prompt gives a good result, save it in a notes file. You will reuse it.

## Worked example

Chen leads a team of six engineers. Tomorrow she has a one-hour meeting with her director to argue for hiring a seventh. She has ten minutes to prepare.

**Move 1 and 2: brief and material.**

```
I lead a team of six software engineers at a mid-sized company.
Tomorrow I have an hour with my director to make the case for one
more hire. She is supportive but under pressure to hold headcount flat.

Here are my rough notes:
- Team supports 4 products, was 2 products eighteen months ago
- On-call load: each person is on call 1 week in 6, incidents up
- Two projects slipped last quarter, both due to people being pulled
  onto support
- One engineer has told me privately they are close to burnout
- A contractor would cost more per month than a hire

Before you help, ask me anything you need to know.
```

The assistant asks three questions: what the director cares about most, whether there are figures for the slippage, and what happens if the answer is no. Chen answers in a few lines. She had not thought about the third one.

**Move 3: steer.**

The assistant produces a structured case. Chen replies:

```
Good structure. Three changes: lead with the delivery risk, not the
team's wellbeing, because that's what she is measured on. Cut it to
five points I can say from memory. And give me the two hardest
questions she is likely to ask, with a suggested answer to each.
```

The second version is tighter. One suggested answer includes the line "industry data shows teams of this size typically support two to three products". Chen did not supply that and cannot verify it.

**Move 4: check and use.**

She asks, "Where did the industry figure come from?" The assistant says it cannot point to a specific source. She deletes the line. She rewrites two of the five points in her own words, copies the result into her notes, and saves the opening prompt for the next time she has to prepare a case.

Total time: nine minutes. She walks in with a clear argument, the two hard questions already thought through, and nothing in her notes that she cannot stand behind.

## Try it on your own work

1. Choose something real you have to do in the next two days: a meeting to prepare for, a message to write, a decision to think through.
2. Open a new chat. Brief the assistant in three or four sentences as you would a new colleague.
3. Add your material, after checking it is safe to share. End with: "Before you help, ask me anything you need to know."
4. Answer its questions. Then steer the result with at least three follow-ups.
5. Check every fact in the final version. Remove anything you did not supply and cannot verify.
6. Use the result, and save the prompt that started it.

## Ignore for now

- Elaborate prompt templates and "magic words". A clear briefing in plain language does most of the work.
- Custom instructions, memory and saved projects. Useful later, once you know what you repeat.
- Voice mode and mobile apps. Learn the conversation first; the channel does not matter.
