# Choosing an assistant, and free versus paid

## Outcome

After this lesson you can pick one assistant to learn on, decide whether you need to pay for it yet, and run a fair test if you ever want to compare.

## Why it matters

New users lose weeks to the question "which AI is best?". They read comparisons, switch tools, and never get good at any of them. The differences between the leading assistants are much smaller than the difference between someone who uses one well and someone who doesn't. Picking one and getting on with it is the better move.

## The idea

**The three general assistants**

ChatGPT (from OpenAI), Claude (from Anthropic) and Gemini (from Google) are three of the most widely used general-purpose assistants. All three can write, summarise, explain, analyse files, search the web and work with images. All three have a free plan and paid plans. Others exist, including Microsoft Copilot, which many companies provide inside Office. Everything in this course applies to those as well.

**How to choose: three questions, in this order**

1. **What does your employer provide or allow?**
   This comes first. If your company has a business account with one of them, use that one for work. It will have stronger privacy terms than a personal account, and you will not be breaking policy. If you do not know the rule, find out before you paste in anything from work.

2. **Where does your work already live?**
   If your day is in Google Docs, Gmail and Sheets, Gemini is built into them. If it is in Microsoft 365, Copilot is. Having the assistant inside the tool you already use removes a lot of copying and pasting.

3. **Which one do you find easiest to work with?**
   If the first two do not decide it, try the same real task in two of them and keep the one whose answers you prefer. This is a matter of taste, like choosing between two capable colleagues.

Then stop choosing. Spend the next month on that one tool. The skills in this course transfer between assistants almost entirely, so nothing is lost if you switch later.

**Free versus paid**

Free plans are good enough to start and to complete most of this course. What a paid personal plan usually buys:

- **More use.** Free plans limit how many messages you can send in a period, and slow down or switch to a weaker model when you hit the limit.
- **Stronger models.** The most capable model is often limited or unavailable on the free plan.
- **More features.** Longer file uploads, deeper research modes, connections to other apps, and agent features tend to arrive on paid plans first.

Paying for a personal plan does not automatically give you stronger privacy. Privacy depends mainly on whether the account is a consumer account or a business account, and on your settings.

**When to pay**

Pay when you hit the limits of the free plan while doing real work, more than once a week. If you never hit them, you do not need to. Personal plans are billed monthly, so you can try one for a month and cancel.

**A fair test**

If you want to compare two assistants, compare them properly:

- Use a real task from your job, not a trick question.
- Give both the identical prompt and the identical files.
- Judge on what matters to you: accuracy, how much editing the result needs, and whether it followed your instructions.
- Repeat with three different tasks. One task tells you very little.

## Worked example

Amara is an HR business partner at a company that has not issued any AI guidance. She wants to pick an assistant.

**Question 1.** She emails IT: "Do we have a company account for any AI assistant, and is there a policy on using them?" The reply: no company account yet, a policy is being written, and for now nothing containing employee data may be put into any AI tool.

**Question 2.** The company runs on Microsoft 365, but Copilot has not been bought. This does not settle it.

**Question 3.** She picks a task with no personal data in it, rewriting a generic job advert, and gives two assistants the same prompt:

```
Rewrite this job advert for a Customer Support Team Lead.

Goals: attract people with 3+ years of experience who are currently
employed, cut it to under 300 words, remove jargon and clichés, and put
the salary range and working pattern in the first three lines.

[advert pasted here]
```

She compares the results on three points. Did it stay under 300 words? Did it put salary and working pattern first, as instructed? How many phrases would she still need to change? One assistant followed every instruction and needed two small edits. The other wrote more engagingly but ran to 380 words. She repeats with two more tasks, an interview question bank and a policy summary, and finds the same pattern.

She chooses the first assistant, stays on the free plan, and puts a note in her calendar to review in a month whether she is hitting its limits.

## In your tool

The names of plans and what they include change often, so check each provider's pricing page for the current details. The general shape has been stable:

- **ChatGPT:** a free plan, personal paid plans, and Business and Enterprise plans for organisations.
- **Claude:** a free plan, personal paid plans, and Team and Enterprise plans for organisations.
- **Gemini:** a free plan with a Google account, paid personal plans, and Gemini inside Google Workspace for organisations.

## Try it on your own work

1. Find out what your employer provides or permits. If there is a written policy, read it. If nobody knows, ask in writing.
2. Note where most of your work lives: Google, Microsoft, or elsewhere.
3. Pick a real task with nothing confidential in it. Run the identical prompt in two assistants.
4. Score each on accuracy, editing needed, and instructions followed.
5. Choose one and commit to it for the next month.

## Ignore for now

- Specialist and niche AI tools. There are thousands. Come back to them once you know what a general assistant cannot do for you.
- Running models on your own computer.
- Online arguments about which model is "smartest" this month.
