# Meetings: notes, actions and follow-ups

## Outcome

After this lesson you can use an assistant to prepare for a meeting, turn what was said into accurate notes and clear actions, and send the follow-up while everyone still remembers the conversation.

## Why it matters

Meetings produce decisions and commitments, and most of that value leaks away afterwards. Nobody took proper notes, or the notes went out three days later, or two people left with different ideas of what was agreed. Taking good notes while taking part is hard, and writing them up is a chore that gets put off. An assistant can do most of this in minutes. Notes are also a record that others will rely on. A wrong note of who agreed to what causes more trouble than no note at all. Recording people, too, has rules attached.

## The idea

### Before the meeting

- **Agenda.** "Here is what we need to settle and who is attending. Draft an agenda for 45 minutes, with a time for each item and the decision needed for each."
- **Briefing.** Paste in the previous notes, the email thread or the document under discussion. "Summarise where things stand, what was agreed last time and what is still open."

### During: getting the raw material

The assistant needs something to work from. There are three sources, from least to most complete.

1. **Your own rough notes.** Typed or scribbled during the meeting, as untidy as you like. These are often enough.
2. **A spoken recap.** Straight after the meeting, dictate two minutes on what was decided and who is doing what, while it is fresh.
3. **A transcript.** Most meeting tools can now transcribe a call and produce notes automatically. Assistants can also work from a transcript or recording you supply.

**Recording has rules**

Before you record or transcribe anyone:

- **Tell people and get their agreement.** In many places this is a legal requirement, and the rules vary by country and region. It is good manners everywhere. Say it at the start: "I'd like to record this so we get accurate notes. Is everyone happy with that?"
- **Follow your organisation's policy** on which tools may be used and where recordings are kept.
- **Think about what is being said.** Some conversations should not be recorded: sensitive personnel matters, legally privileged discussions, anything a participant asks to keep off the record.

### After: notes people will use

Do not ask for "a summary of the meeting". Ask for the three things people need:

```
From the [notes / transcript] below, produce meeting notes with three
sections:

1. Decisions: what was decided, in one line each.
2. Actions: a table of what, who and by when. Only include actions
   someone actually agreed to. If no owner or date was stated, write
   "not agreed". Don't assign one.
3. Open questions: what was raised and not settled.

Use only what is in the notes. Don't add anything that wasn't said.
If something is unclear, list it at the end for me to check.
```

The instruction "if no owner or date was stated, write 'not agreed'" is the important one. Without it, the assistant fills every row of the table, and people get assigned tasks they never accepted.

**Then the follow-up**

"Draft a follow-up email to the attendees with the decisions and the action table. Under 150 words before the table. Ask them to reply by tomorrow if anything is wrong."

Send it the same day. Asking for corrections turns your notes into an agreed record.

### How meeting notes go wrong

1. **Wrong speaker.** A transcript mixes up who said what, especially with similar voices or people sharing a microphone.
2. **Invented owners and dates.** An action is assigned to whoever was speaking nearby, with a plausible deadline.
3. **A discussion recorded as a decision.** "We talked about moving the launch" becomes "Agreed to move the launch".
4. **Lost qualifications.** "Probably yes, subject to pricing" becomes "yes".
5. **Mis-heard names, numbers and terms.** Product names, people's names and figures are where transcription errors cluster.
6. **Missing the unspoken.** Notes capture words. They do not capture hesitation, or the fact that someone visibly disagreed and said nothing.

### Checking the notes

You were in the meeting, which makes you the check.

- **Read every decision.** Was it actually decided, or only discussed?
- **Read every action.** Did that person agree to it, and by that date?
- **Check names, numbers and dates** against what you remember and your own notes.
- **Look for what is missing.** Is there something important that you remember and the notes do not have?
- **Add what the words did not carry**, where it matters: "Sam had reservations about the timeline."

Five minutes is enough. Never send automatic notes unread.

## Worked example

Marta is an account director at a firm that supplies training programmes to large companies. She has a quarterly review call with her biggest client: their head of learning, a procurement manager and two others. The contract is up for renewal in three months.

**Before.** She pastes in last quarter's notes and the recent email thread and asks for a briefing: where things stand, what is open, and what she is likely to be asked. The briefing reminds her that the client raised completion rates last time and that she promised figures. She gets them ready.

**During.** At the start of the call she says, "I'd like to record this so that I can send accurate notes afterwards. Is that all right with everyone?" All four agree. Her meeting tool records and produces a transcript.

**After.** She gives the transcript to her company's approved assistant, with the notes prompt. In two minutes she has:

> **Decisions**
> - Client will renew for a further 12 months.
> - The leadership module will be redesigned for the next cohort.
>
> **Actions**
> | What | Who | By when |
> |---|---|---|
> | Send completion-rate data by department | Marta | 18 Oct |
> | Share revised pricing proposal | Marta | 25 Oct |
> | Confirm budget for the redesign | Priyanka | not agreed |
> | Provide list of participants for next cohort | Dev | 1 Nov |
>
> **Open questions**
> - Whether the programme can be delivered in two regional sites.
>
> **To check**
> - A figure of "sixty-eight" or "sixty" per cent was mentioned for completion; the transcript is unclear.

**Check.** Marta reads it against her memory of the call.

- *"Client will renew for a further 12 months."* This is wrong, and it is the most important line. What the head of learning actually said was that they were "inclined to renew, subject to seeing the revised pricing". That is a statement of intention with a condition attached. If the notes went out as written, procurement would object at once, or the head of learning would feel that words had been put in her mouth. Marta moves it to open questions: "Renewal: client inclined to renew, subject to revised pricing (due 25 Oct)."
- *"Provide list of participants — Dev."* She remembers that it was Priyanka who offered this. Dev had been speaking just before. She corrects the owner.
- *"Confirm budget for the redesign — Priyanka — not agreed."* This is correct. No date was given. She leaves it and decides to ask for one in the email.
- *The figure.* It was 68%. She had it in her own notes.
- *Missing.* The procurement manager was quiet throughout and asked only one question, about payment terms. That is not in the notes, because it was a brief exchange. Marta adds a private note for herself to follow up with him separately.

**Follow-up.** She asks for a short email with the corrected decisions and action table, ending: "Please reply by tomorrow if I have anything wrong. Priyanka, could you let me know when you expect to confirm the redesign budget?"

She reads it once and sends it forty minutes after the call ended. Priyanka replies with a date. Nobody disputes the record.

The assistant produced the structure in two minutes. Of the two "decisions", one was not a decision, and one action had the wrong owner. Marta caught both because she had been in the meeting and read the notes before sending them.

## In your tool

- **Meeting tools:** Zoom, Microsoft Teams and Google Meet each offer built-in transcription and automatic notes on eligible plans. Participants are normally notified when these are switched on.
- **Assistants:** ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini can all work from a pasted transcript or your own notes. Some can also take an audio file.

Which features you have depends on your plan and on what your organisation has enabled.

## Try it on your own work

1. Before your next meeting, give the assistant the background and ask for a briefing and the questions you are likely to face.
2. Take rough notes, or dictate a two-minute recap straight afterwards. If you record, ask first.
3. Use the notes prompt: decisions, actions with "not agreed" where no owner or date was given, and open questions.
4. Check every decision and every action against what you remember.
5. Send a follow-up the same day and ask for corrections.
6. Notice what the notes missed that you saw in the room.

## Ignore for now

- Recording every meeting by default. Choose the meetings where an accurate record matters.
- Tools that join meetings on your behalf when you are absent.
